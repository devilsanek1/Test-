# How feature studios draw storyboards and story reels: fidelity, values, line, staging, camera marks, speed (research lane, 2026-10-26)

Feeds the G3 sketch-animatic look (`video/src/v3/SketchReel.tsx`) after the owner's note: "the sketch is making it confusing; keep it
very simple, maybe no colours, like an actual drawing". Tags: **VERIFIED** = several sources agree or a primary page says it;
**REPORTED** = one secondary source or a search summary; **UNCERTAIN** = unconfirmed, vendor-only or generated text. WebFetch was
blocked (DNS / proxy 403) on every page, so web claims come from search-result text; [n] points to the source list at the end.

## 0. The rules in brief
1. **Black line on paper, plus at most two greys, no hue.** Studios board in black and white to work fast; colour is designed later (§1).
2. **One panel, one idea.** New drawing when the composition, action or angle changes, not for every sentence (§2, §3).
3. **Three depth planes, three values:** far = paper, middle = light grey; the beat's subject gets the strongest contrast in the frame (§4).
4. **Line weight is the hierarchy:** subject heavy, middle medium, background thin and lighter; far things become a plain silhouette (§4).
5. **Stage it in silhouette:** the key pose or prop reads as a filled dark shape; no hand across the face or body; no overlaps on it (§3).
6. **Draw only what carries the beat;** everything on screen is read as meaningful. Words in the picture are a last resort (§3, §6).
7. **Camera moves are marks first** (edge arrows, four corner arrows for a zoom); a real move only where the move tells the story (§5).
8. **The reel is a film:** voice, temp sound and timing always; screened, torn up, redrawn many times; drawings are disposable (§2).

## 1. Fidelity: rough, monochrome, fast
- Pixar's Mark Andrews (head of story, The Incredibles): boards are black and white because "we want to work very, very, very fast"
  and use little "computer juice"; shots, motion and editing must be settled before layout, animation and lighting start [1]. VERIFIED.
- Pixar reels are "basic black and white cartoon versions of the film", from scanned 3½ x 8 in panels pinned on 4 x 8 ft boards
  (Harley Jessup) [2]. REPORTED. Enrico Casarosa (Ratatouille): reels are "roughly drawn but with temp voice, sound and music";
  the job should be called "re-storyboarding" [4]. REPORTED. Disney artists "work from rough to fine, creating clear drawings" [5]. REPORTED.
- Andrews calls heavily animated boards "a waste of time" taken from story [21]. UNCERTAIN (excerpt cut off). Cleaning up a rough
  can drain its energy (Mike Milo) [14]. REPORTED.
- Job ads: "quick thumbnail sketches through to finished detailed storyboard panels" (Pixar); "draw convincingly and quickly,
  capturing the essence of characters and environments" (Disney) [15]. REPORTED.

## 2. Volume, speed and the screening loop
- Drawings per film: A Bug's Life 27,500 (Joe Ranft: "used some and discarded many more") [8]; Finding Nemo 43,536, Cars 47,000,
  Ratatouille 72,000 [2]; WALL-E over 125,000 [3]; Ralph Breaks the Internet over 200,000 [5]. VERIFIED as an order of magnitude,
  REPORTED per figure. Inside Out ~127,000, "around 23 drawings per second of film" (Josh Cooley Q&A, retold on a forum) [9]. UNCERTAIN.
  These count every version drawn, not panels on screen.
- No studio source gives on-screen panels per second. Toon Boom Storyboard Pro times a new panel at 1 s by default [23]; the working
  rule is one panel per visual beat [24]. REPORTED. Derek Thompson (WALL-E): "we essentially draw the movie, redraw the movie,
  redraw it and redraw it, over and over again for many, many years" [3]. REPORTED.
- The whole film is screened as a reel with staff scratch dialogue, temp music and effects; Pixar "never" goes to animation without
  temp dialogue [6][7]. VERIFIED. The Brain Trust / Story Trust watch a film 6-8 times across boards, animatics and animation [10];
  about 3-4 months per pass [11]. REPORTED.
- Ed Catmull: "early on, all of our movies suck" [25]. VERIFIED. Andrew Stanton: be wrong as fast as you can [26]. VERIFIED (wording
  varies). Pete Docter (Inside Out): rewrite, reboard, screen again and again; a fresh audience is key [11]. REPORTED.
- Boards find the story: Up's "Married Life" was scripted with dialogue; once boarding began, head of story Ronnie del Carmen pushed
  for none, and the music carries it [12]. REPORTED.
- Pitching: the artist projects the drawings and performs the voices for a story crew of 6-10 (Fawn Veerasunthorn, Disney) [5][15];
  Zootopia had 12-15 story artists (Josie Trinidad) [15]. REPORTED.
- Fast tools: Disney's StoryPad (iPad, vector, multi-layer) [5] VERIFIED; Foundry Flix at DreamWorks (HTTYD 3) and Laika (Kubo) [27]
  REPORTED (vendor). Brad Bird's Iron Giant reels used Director, then After Effects, to test camera, staging and timing early [28]. REPORTED.

## 3. What makes a board readable
- **One thing per panel:** Ranft: a good panel communicates a single thing, staged to make that one point [8]. REPORTED.
- **One idea at a time, the eye led:** "Look at this, now look at this, and now look at this"; motion draws the eye in a still frame,
  a still thing draws it in a busy one [13]. VERIFIED (two teaching pages restating The Illusion of Life).
- **Silhouette:** early Mickey had no greys, so actions had to read in silhouette; "it is always better to show the action in
  silhouette"; keep the hand away from the face [16]. REPORTED (handout quoting the book). Walt Stanchfield's Disney lectures have
  chapters "Simplicity for the Sake of Clarity" and "Line and Silhouette" [17]. VERIFIED.
- **Subtract:** "Anything you add will be read as significant"; remove what is irrelevant or can't be identified [18]. REPORTED.
  Mark Kennedy (Disney head of story, Tangled): "An artist emphasizes some things and de-emphasizes other things" [19]. REPORTED.
- **Clarity comes from the drawing:** "good silhouettes, clear poses and good solid acting"; staging shows the viewer "what you WANT
  them to see" (Mike Milo) [14]. REPORTED. Readable action outranks texture, colour and rendering [24]. REPORTED.
- **Business before presentation:** settle the gags, situations and emotions, then how to show them (Bird on the Disney method) [28];
  Pixar boards from a "beat outline", "a map of the characters' emotional changes" [10]. REPORTED.

## 4. Values, line weight and depth
- Feature boards are monochrome [1][2]; no studio source fixes a number of greys. UNCERTAIN.
- Illustrators give each depth plane one value range (white, grey, black, any order); muddy values kill shapes at small size [20].
  REPORTED. A storyboard toning method: light ground, one dark tone, then highlights; the darkest grey doubles as contour and cast
  shadow [20]. REPORTED.
- Line weight: heavy contours in front, light behind, dropping out in the far distance; background lines thinner than character lines
  so the set doesn't compete; distant things simplify into lighter silhouettes [22]. REPORTED (illustration consensus).
- Framed Ink (Marcos Mateu-Mestre, DreamWorks/Sony boards) builds the image from atmosphere, lighting and chiaroscuro, to make the
  audience "feel" the story rather than be told it [29]. VERIFIED (contents, publisher text). Prepare to Board! (Nancy Beiman):
  characters develop with the story; boards catch problems "before they cost time and money" [30]. REPORTED (blurbs only).

## 5. Marking the camera
- Arrows point the way the camera travels ("camera pan" label if needed); tilt = curved arrows on the side; zoom = four corner arrows
  pointing in or out (or around one area); L-shaped arrow for tilt-then-pan; camera marks at the panel edge, subject-motion arrows
  inside, next to what moves [31][32]. VERIFIED (two guides agree); artists vary. Dolly: thick arrows [32]. REPORTED.
- Push-in end frame as a smaller box inside the panel: common practice in my experience, no source found. UNCERTAIN.
  Pan: one panel wider than the frame, a crop window slides across; it must compose well all along [24]. UNCERTAIN.
- In a reel, moves are real but crude (Iron Giant's After Effects reels [28]); they exist to test story and timing.

## 6. What they deliberately leave out
Colour [1]; rendering and texture [24]; animation inside the boards [21]; set detail that doesn't serve the beat [18]; dialogue the
pictures already carry [12]; polish of any kind before the screening has passed [3][4][25].

## 7. What this means for SketchReel at G3 (our application, not sourced)
- Fills: `paper` + one light grey + one dark, the dark only on the beat's subject. Retire `warm`, `cold`, `pop` at G3.
- Strokes: three widths (subject ~7, middle ~4, background ~2 at 60 % ink) instead of 4 everywhere.
- Shots: one per visual beat; hold one strong image longer instead of adding elements. No parallax at G3; cuts by default; a camera
  move only where it is the meaning (push-in on the reveal), with an arrow mark in its first 0.5 s.
- Figures: a posed silhouette (head, body line, arms) whose pose reads at phone size; a face only when the emotion is the beat.
  Labels in the picture only when the drawing can't say it, at most one per shot.
- Checks before the owner sees it: (a) fill every shape flat dark: does the subject still read? (b) at 25 % size, is the one idea
  clear in about a second?
