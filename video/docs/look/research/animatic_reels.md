# Story reels in feature animation: what the animatic stage locks, fakes, and omits (research for G3, 2026-10-26)

How Pixar, Disney, DreamWorks, Sony, Aardman and Ghibli use the story reel (Leica reel / animatic) as the gate before
layout and animation. **VERIFIED** = primary page or several sources agree; **REPORTED** = one secondary source;
"derived" = our own arithmetic from a sourced number.

## 1. What a reel is, and what it locks
- A reel is EVERY board of the film cut to scratch dialogue, temp music and SFX, at full runtime: "a visual rough draft
  of the whole thing" — a 90-minute film gets 90-minute reels [6]. Editorial builds and owns it ("keepers of the cut");
  making story reels is editorial's whole job "for the first couple of years on the film" (Axel Geddes, Toy Story 4 editor) [1][2]. VERIFIED.
- Reel approval is per sequence, before layout, and it locks the cut: shot order, what each shot shows, shot lengths,
  dialogue, and the emotional intent — "This is the version of the movie we're gonna make for this three minutes" [1]. VERIFIED.
- The reel is the document that communicates "shot placement, timing, and camera movement" downstream [21]. REPORTED.
- Decided LATER: the real camera (Disney layout "builds the first 3D version of the storyboard" and rough-animates it [23],
  VERIFIED; layout and blocking can overrule the board's camera and timing [11], REPORTED); acting detail (animation);
  final voices; colour and light (a separate colour-script pass, kept out of boards so story can "work very, very, very
  fast" [24], VERIFIED). Classic Disney inserted a "workbook" pass translating approved boards into camera/BG plans [22]. REPORTED.
- What directors judge a reel on: whether the ideas communicate ("to test out whether the ideas are being communicated" [1]);
  content, timing and jokes, with camera re-blocked at this stage "if the camera work seems 'clunky'" (Dan Lee) [14]. VERIFIED.

## 2. How reels fake motion cheaply
- Default grammar: held drawings cut on the dialogue. Camera is faked with pans, scales/zooms and dissolves over stills
  (the "boardomatic" level); real drawn motion — characters moving, dynamic transitions — is added only for key actions
  (the "animatic" level) [9][10]. VERIFIED (multiple guides agree on the two levels).
- Scale and space are faked with overlapping pans across one oversized drawing (a concert crowd built from 22 frames) [9]. REPORTED.
- Where motion matters, pros spend drawings, not rigs: TV-style boards are "very animated and dence with poses" (sic);
  a DreamWorks/Illumination board artist describes "presentation boards that can be edited tightly together to create an
  animatic ... Lots of drawings and some after effects work" (Croods) [13]. VERIFIED (primary page).
- Volume: story artists deliver "100 to 400 to 900 boards (drawings) for a sequence" [1] (a sequence ≈ 2-4 min). VERIFIED.
  Whole-film totals across all drafts: "over four-thousand storyboard drawings" (Toy Story 2 era) [7] vs ~50,000-125,000
  (WALL-E era) [8] — sources conflict, but both say boards are redrawn constantly, in bulk. REPORTED.
  Derived: 100-900 boards over a 2-4 min sequence of ~30-80 shots ≈ **3-15 drawings per shot** — 1-3 held poses for a
  dialogue shot, dozens for an action beat.
- Sound scratch: temp dialogue is recorded "with our friends and neighbors around the studio" [1]; on WALL-E the
  filmmakers "never go to the final animation process without recording temporary dialogue" [25]. The rule for spending:
  "Ideally, we don't animate any of that scratch" [1] — animation dollars wait for locked sound. VERIFIED.
- Aardman records audio first and animates to dialogue; Nick Park: "we edit the whole thing with music, we do scratch
  voices ourselves as we test out the script" (Early Man) [15][16]. VERIFIED.

## 3. Iteration: how reels get screened and fixed
- "Early on, all of our movies suck"; the job is going "from suck to not-suck" by iterating reels (Ed Catmull). The
  Braintrust watches the current reel and gives candid notes but "has no authority" — the director decides [3]. VERIFIED.
- Cadence: the Disney/Pixar story Trusts watch a film "an average of six to eight times in various stages of storyboards,
  animatics, and animation", meeting "to pull the story apart, figure out what is not working, and put the story back
  together again"; motto "be wrong fast" [5]. REPORTED. Toy Story 4: "about eight or 10 screenings" over 4.5 years [1]. VERIFIED.
- Between screenings the fix ranges from "a whole 'page one rewrite'" to "tweaking and turning of knobs" [1]. A failing
  sequence is identified at the whole-film screening, re-boarded by story, re-cut by editorial; approved sequences stay. VERIFIED.
- Pixar averages ~8 full visual rough drafts of the film, going "back and forth between the 'reels' and new drafts of the
  written script" for 2-3 years [6]. REPORTED. "The movie works in reels" = every sequence plays, with scratch, at full
  runtime — only then is it released to layout/animation, because animation is the expensive, slow step [1][3].

## 4. What reels deliberately omit, and why that is safe
- Omitted: colour (separate colour script [24]), inbetweens (held poses only), effects, backgrounds beyond staging,
  professional voices, final music. "Full animation isn't the point" [10]. VERIFIED.
- Why safe: the variables being judged — order, timing, clarity, emotional intent, jokes — are fully carried by drawing +
  voice + sound. "If a film's Story Reel 'plays' in pencil poses and non-professional performances, it will sing in
  Technicolor" [4]. REPORTED.
- The limit of omission: anything whose MEANING is motion, space or quantity does not survive being left out. Boards can
  cheat staging ("many people use a camera move because their original staging does not work for the entire scene" [12],
  REPORTED), and a cheat surfaces only in layout, which then overrules the board [11][23]. A reel must therefore carry a
  placeholder for every such shot (a pan, a pose run, a drawn crowd), not a note.

## 5. Documented costs of a reel that lied (or was skipped)
- Toy Story 2: early reels were "disastrous" while production was already underway; the story was reconceived and the
  film substantially remade in its final ~9 months [18][3]. REPORTED.
- Finding Dory, "Sleep Swimming": a sequence was FINISHED in animation, then cut when act one had to shorten — a story
  problem that outlived the reel stage cost a fully animated sequence [1]. VERIFIED. Same film: the prologue was broken
  into memories late, retrofitting the rest of the movie [1]. Toy Story 4's third act was rewritten ~6 months before release [1]. VERIFIED.
- LEGO Movie: Lord & Miller test-screened unfinished cuts and found screening "a movie that's only half animated" hard —
  mixed fidelity skews the read, the finished parts play charming against boards [19]. REPORTED.
- Ghibli is the cautionary counter-model: production starts "while the storyboards are still developing"; Miyazaki
  himself calls it "a dangerous way to make an animation film" [17]. VERIFIED (his own interview).
- Spider-Verse: a full early animatic (Alberto Mielgo) was used to settle tone and visual direction before production [20]. REPORTED.

## What this means for our G3
1. G3 is a full-runtime reel against the LOCKED take: every one of our 30-45 s covered by a drawn panel, every transition performed (cut/dissolve/match drawn in), never written as a note [6][1].
2. Lock at G3 and freeze: shot order, each shot's content, lengths, framing/shot type, and both end frames of every camera move; defer to G5 only polish variables: colour, faces beyond the beat, easing feel, texture [1][11].
3. Budget drawings per shot like editorial, not one panel per shot: 2-3 poses on a held dialogue shot (pose changes on the beat words), 6-12 drawings on the 1-3 shots whose meaning IS motion — for ~15 shots that is ~40-80 distinct drawings per G3 reel, pros ship 100-900 per sequence [1][13].
4. Kill the v3 "empty at G5" surprise with density placeholders: every panel shows its FINAL element count (a crowd = that many 3-stroke grey figures, a shelf = that many slots) — omit sharpness and detail, never presence; board-lint checks a per-shot `density` count against the G5 set plan.
5. Fake the camera the boardomatic way: draw panels 120-140 % of frame so pans/scales/dissolves run on stills; a move that cannot be faked on a still (parallax rise, reveal) gets its two end frames drawn and the cheap in-between animated — nothing else gets real motion [9][10].
6. Add a per-shot CHEAT line to the notes strip: name what this board is faking (speed, weight, crowd, room) and what placeholder stands in for it; a known cheat with no placeholder blocks the gate — cheats are what layout discovers too late [12][11].
7. Scratch-complete the sound: G3 plays with the locked VO plus placeholder SFX/music stings on the beats that G5 will score; never spend G4+ polish on a shot whose words might still change ("we don't animate scratch") [1][25].
8. Screen the WHOLE reel every fix round on the owner's phone (pros re-watch the full film 6-10 times), map each note to shot IDs, re-board only failing shots, keep approved shots frozen [5][1][3].
9. Expect and budget "page-one" verdicts at G3: a reel exists so the owner can say "THAT didn't work" while it costs drawings, not builds — a story note that survives past G3 costs a finished shot (Sleep Swimming) [1][18].
10. Two-pass judgment at G3: sound-off pass for readability (existing checklist), sound-on pass for pacing, intent and jokes — both pass before any G4 build starts; G4 may still overrule board timing by <=10 %, anything bigger goes back to G3 [14][11].
