# The rough pass: loose drawings that already carry 100% of the motion (research, 2026-10-26)

Lane: how 2D animators make a rough pass that is the final film's motion, minus the detail. **VERIFIED** = primary page or
several sources agree; **REPORTED** = one secondary source. Numbers with no source are our own choices for our frame.

## 1. Pose test / blocking: timing is judged from held keys, before any inbetween exists
- Traditional 2D order: keys are drawn and shot on a line tester first, each key HELD for the estimated number of
  inbetween frames; the resulting movie is the "pose test" / "key test", and it already plays at roughly the right speed
  and length [1]. VERIFIED. Williams' order (secondhand, ASK pp. 64-67): story/dialogue sets the timing, then key poses,
  then extremes, then breakdowns [2]. REPORTED.
- 3D blocking is the same practice: stepped poses (no interpolation) so timing and spacing read undiluted; splining too
  early "dilutes the timing"; blocking is proof of concept, so smoothness between poses is irrelevant to its goal [3][4]. VERIFIED (practitioner sources agree).
- A scene is ready for cleanup only when the performance reads consistently, the timing supports the beat and the major
  motion problems are solved in rough; creeping shoulders, volume chatter and sliding feet are far cheaper to fix rough,
  before clean lines make every revision labor-intensive [5][6]. REPORTED (one publisher, several pages).
- No source states "if it doesn't work in rough it never will" verbatim; the documented equivalent: the rough animator
  OWNS the performance, cleanup only makes it readable — cleanup preserves motion, it never creates it [5][7]. VERIFIED.
- Eric Larson (Disney), on how rough is too rough: "the REAL answer has to do with clarity" — a rough is right when
  nobody downstream can misread its intent [8]. VERIFIED (primary page, Deja's blog).

## 2. What a rough keeps vs drops
- Keeps: construction, pose, line of action, VOLUME, contact points. Milt Kahl drew body volumes "in a scribbly but
  accurate way"; in fast scenes he omitted facial details entirely and let the assistant finish them [8]. VERIFIED.
- Volume drift is the #1 cleanup problem: heads/hands/torsos changing size between frames look fine as stills and
  "chatter" on playback — fix volumes in rough, before refining [5][7]. REPORTED.
- Drops: cleanup line quality, faces (fast action), interior detail, texture. Grim Natwick worked in two passes: a
  scribble pass for energy, a second pass to solidify volumes (via a Deja commenter) [8]. REPORTED.
- Order: clean/approve the keys first, then the major breakdowns; judge the shot in motion (flip/scrub), never frame by
  frame [7]. REPORTED. A rough is cheap to discard; a cleaned drawing is not — that is where the speed lives [5]. REPORTED.
- No documented rough:cleanup time ratio found. Structural evidence that cleanup is the expensive half: Disney split
  animator / assistant / inbetweener by 1931 and moved animators to roughs-only with pencil tests in 1932; approved
  scenes then pass to a whole cleanup department [9]. VERIFIED.

## 3. Line boil: the sketch aesthetic kept in finished films
- Boil = cycling several tracings of the same drawing so held elements wobble instead of dying; limited animation holds
  the drawing for still parts and redraws only what moves [10][11]. VERIFIED.
- Loop size: usually 3 tracings (the classic; Ed, Edd n Eddy used three) up to 3-8 drawings; Squigglevision used 5
  [11][12][13]. VERIFIED (sources agree on the 3+ loop). Rate: trace 2-4 copies and loop at ~8 fps — a new drawing
  every 3 frames at 24 fps; some tools default ~12 fps [13]. REPORTED. No source quantifies amplitude.
- Readability rule (Hodgson's *Dogs*, 1981; *Gertie*, 1914): the central/moving figure gets the LEAST outline boil and
  the subtlest shading so the narrative stays clear; surroundings (leaves, sea lines, background) boil more; Gertie's
  outline was carefully registered while the foliage moved [11]. VERIFIED. Low-level boil keeps the whole frame feeling
  alive; intense boil reads as unease or loss of control [11]. VERIFIED.
- Joanna Quinn: all hand-drawn on paper, "I draw quite quickly... what makes it feel alive is that I don't tidy it up
  too much"; mistakes are rubbed into the drawing, not erased; key drawings are kept visible while inbetweens serve the
  movement; a scene decided as a thumbnail stays true to that sketch all the way to the final frame [14][15]. VERIFIED.
- Bill Plympton: low drawing count, strong static poses over classic timing; rough hatching exploits the boil to set
  mood; pencil drawings are photographed with "no painstaking cel cleanup"; ~30,000 cels for the 69-min *The Tune*
  (~7 drawings/s average for a finished feature) [16]. REPORTED. a-ha's *Take On Me*: ~3,000 rotoscoped pencil frames,
  16 weeks — the boiling sketch read as finished style in a hit video [17]. VERIFIED.

## 4. How roughs suggest density cheaply
- Crowds: see the crowd as ONE collective shape (height, width, density) and draw that mass silhouette first; heads form
  horizontal bands drawn as simple rounded shapes; back rows are part-hidden, a stepped silhouette [18]. REPORTED.
- Foliage/architecture: establish the big mass, suggest leaves rather than draw them; a dark mass (doorway, awning
  shadow, foliage) organizes a drawing better than another layer of tiny details [19]. REPORTED.
- Selective detail: detail and contrast only at the focal point; marks get lighter, simpler and less defined with
  distance from it; background characters are silhouettes [18][19][20]. VERIFIED (tutorial sources agree).
- So density is promised by MASS, not by count: a filled scribble-mass placeholder carries "this space is full" at
  thumbnail cost, and the focal subject stays the only sharp thing.

## 5. Smears, multiples and stepped motion at low drawing counts
- A smear replaces a full set of inbetweens with 1-3 frames of stretched/blurred drawing between two extremes (*The
  Dover Boys*, 1942); limited animation = characters either hold or move so fast the motion reads as blur [21][22][23]. VERIFIED.
- Frame economy: on ones = 24 drawings/s, only for very fast moves; on twos = 12/s, the common standard; slow moves on
  threes or fours (8 / 6 per s) [24][5]. VERIFIED for twos as standard; threes/fours REPORTED (uncited on the page).
- A finished film reads as fluid INTENDED motion well below 12/s when poses are strong and fast transits are smears
  (Plympton averages ~7/s [16]); a pose test reads the same motion at 1-4 held poses/s [1]. REPORTED.
- The ladder: pose test (keys held, 1-4 poses/s) → rough pass (keys + breakdowns + smears, stepped) → polish adds
  inbetweens/cleanup. Each rung keeps 100% of the timing decisions of the rung before [1][3][5]. VERIFIED.

## What this means for our G3
1. G3 is a pose test, not a slideshow: each shot's action is 2-4 held key poses popping on their words (held 0.3-1.5 s), stepped, never an eased slide — timing reads undiluted, like stepped blocking.
2. Motion contract: every move that will exist at G5 exists at G3 as >= 2 stepped drawings (key → breakdown/smear → key); a G5 move with no G3 placeholder is banned, and G5 adds no new motion — only cleanup, colour, easing.
3. Fast transits are ONE smear drawing (silhouette stretched along the path) held 2-3 frames between the two keys — cheapest possible "this will be fluid" signal; add a `pose: 'smear'` figure variant.
4. Boil layer on every held element: cycle 3 jitter seeds, new seed every 4 frames at 24 fps equivalent (every 5 at the 30 fps reel, every 8-10 at 60 fps), amplitude 2-3 px (our number); SketchReel gets `boil: {seeds: 3, hold: n}` on by default for holds.
5. Boil gradient = focus gradient (*Dogs* rule): subject boils least (amplitude ~1-2 px), mid plane more, far plane most; never boil a line someone must read (captions, numbers, notes strip: static).
6. Density placeholders: a crowd/foliage/rack/skyline is ONE mass silhouette (counts as 1 lint element) plus 3-5 suggestion marks inside; empty paper at G3 means an empty scene at G5 — the v3 "way too empty" note gets caught here, because promised density is visible as mass.
7. Detail gradient: interior marks only on the subject; mid plane mass + 1-2 marks; far plane mass only — the focal point is the only sharp thing in the panel.
8. Volume/contact lint: a subject's bbox and its ground-contact y stay within 10% across a shot's poses unless the change is the idea (volume chatter is the #1 cleanup bug; catch it at G3).
9. Faces and hands drop in motion poses (Kahl); an emotion face appears only on the held acting beat; mitten hands always.
10. Two-pass build per panel (Natwick): scribble pass for energy/placement judged on the contact sheet, volume pass only on panels that survive — a rough is cheap to discard, so discard at pass 1.
11. Drawing budget for a 40 s reel: ~60-120 distinct drawings total (2-4 poses x ~15 shots + smears + 3-seed boils), not 12/s; fluidity comes from pose strength + smears, like Plympton's ~7/s features.
12. The G3 review question changes: the owner is approving MOTION, TIMING and DENSITY from the rough (cleanup preserves, never creates) — the MP4 note to the owner says exactly that, so "scenes too empty / smoothness not there" is a G3 note, not a G5 surprise.
