## 0. Bottom line

1. **Two phases, one place.** For the first ~0.2-0.5 s after a picture or a cut appears, low-level salience aims the
   eye: the first saccades go to the screen centre, to motion onsets and to the strongest local contrast [AT5], [AT6],
   [AT12], [AT15]. From ~0.5 s, meaning leads: objects, people and words explain where people look, and once meaning is
   controlled, salience adds nothing of its own (meaning 53 % of the variance of attention maps [AT7]). A frame wins when
   the subject is BOTH its salience peak at 0.3 s and its meaning at 1 s: the subject, the story light, the hardest value
   step, the beat's one onset and the caption's referent in one place. The owner's reels show what happens otherwise: in
   12 of 20 one-subject examples a brighter patch elsewhere beat the subject the lines pointed at (`reels.md` §0); the
   evidence says that patch takes the first saccade.
2. **The levers, ranked by evidence and size of effect** (§3): **L1 motion onset** (a start, an entry, a growth toward
   us, animate motion) > **L2 words** > **L3 people and animals** > **L4 the screen centre** (re-found at every cut) >
   **L5 value contrast and the brightest area** > **L6 one unique thing in a uniform field** (and clutter's cost) > **L7
   words and picture together** > **L8 coherence** > **L9 size** > **L10 a sound on the change** > **L11 pointers that
   converge** (vanishing point, sightlines, lines) > **L12 selective detail** > **L13 the light as the cue** > **L14
   emotion** > **L15 pacing**. The classic placement grids (thirds, the golden spiral and triangle) have no evidence as
   gaze guides [AT31], [S3], [S4]: they are tools of balance and proportion.
3. **Motion onset, not motion, takes the eye.** A thing that STARTS to move captures attention, and then is found
   regardless of how many other things are on screen; steady motion and stopping do not capture [AT10]. Looming
   captures, receding does not [AT11]; self-propelled, turning (animate) motion captures more than mechanical motion
   [AT14]; in video, flicker and motion predict where every viewer looks at once [AT13]. So each beat has ONE onset, the
   subject's change (a stroke drawing on, a pop, a tick, a light coming on), started in the golden box; supporting life
   moves steadily or not at all near it. `CRAFT.md` §b ("B: constant speed, no pops"; none enters in the 0.4 s around a
   join) is exactly what the evidence asks.
4. **Words are the second magnet, and we already spend it on the captions.** Text draws 11.1 times the fixations of a
   size- and position-matched region, almost as much as a face (16.6x) [AT16], even when it is upside down or in an
   unknown script [AT17]; subtitles are read automatically, taking ~30 % of viewing time [AT21], [V9]. So words live in
   the band and in the one number; nothing letter-like anywhere else in the picture; and since a caption page is itself
   an onset, the picture's event leads its word by 0.1-0.3 s (words with the picture: d ≈ 1.2-1.3 in explainer
   research [AT34]).
5. **People and animals are magnets whatever their tier.** Saccades reach faces from 100-110 ms [AT4]; human
   silhouettes and hands break through inattention [AT18]; added people and animals go unnoticed 11 % of the time
   against 34 % for objects [AT19]. Our people are seen from behind with the LASH face, which keeps the strongest cue
   (a face looking out) off the screen; a body still pulls. So a person is the subject or stays tier B (soft, small,
   steady), and the "you" is turned so its own pull routes into the focal (COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 7).
6. **The first second after a swipe, in numbers** (§4): the page settles at ~0.4 s (measured, `vertical.md` §4); the
   gist of a scene comes from 19-67 ms of looking [AT1]; the first saccade follows ~0.15-0.3 s after the picture is
   there, aimed by the centre and by salience; the subject and the first caption line are read by ~1-1.2 s; the
   stay-or-swipe decision falls at ~1.5-2 s (1.7 s per mobile feed item [V4]; ~1.5-2 s of active attention on fast
   feeds [AT55]). So the subject's bold onset peaks at 0.4-0.7 s in the golden box with its sound, the subject changes
   again at ~1.2-1.7 s (rewarding the eye's return from the caption), and reality overtakes the expectation at ~2 s
   (DIRECTION rule 9).
7. **Clutter has a measured price; fullness does not.** Dense perceptual features (edges, colours, luminance
   variation) cut attention to the key element and liking, while an elaborate design of objects raises both (249 ads
   eye-tracked [AT26]); feelings on smaller faces are read more slowly, more so in clutter, and films hold such shots
   longer [AT28]; search slows as clutter rises [AT24]. Our "full but calm" world is the evidence-based one: the many
   things as tone-on-tone texture (low feature contrast), few named objects, and a clear ring of ~150-220 px around the
   subject (the crowding distance at the 4-6 degree drop from the words [AT25]).
8. **For an explainer, the strongest numbers come from multimedia-learning meta-analyses:** words and picture at the
   same time d ≈ 1.22-1.30, near each other d ≈ 0.79-1.10, extraneous material cut d ≈ 0.70-0.86, cues d ≈ 0.41-0.46
   [AT34]; interesting-but-irrelevant extras cost g ≈ -0.33, more when the viewer cannot set the pace, which is video
   [AT35]; a spotlight cue in an animation improved understanding even of what it did not light [AT36]; cues that are
   part of the animation's own flow beat arrows [AT37]. Our captions over the focal, visuals leading their words, calm
   tiers and the story light as the cue each have that support.
9. **Holding attention: event rate, not cut rate** (§5). Camera changes inside a scene raised arousal and memory up to
   very fast rates [AT46]; fast pace plus arousing content overloads viewers [AT46]; continuity cuts that coincide with
   a motion onset go unseen a third of the time [AT42]; a tightly made film synchronises viewers' brains (inter-subject
   correlation over 65 % of the cortex for a Hitchcock episode, under 5 % for an unedited shot of a park [AT43]);
   modern films have shorter shots with more motion, which their measurers read as tighter control of attention
   [AT45]. Short-form practice says "a refresh every ~2-5 s" (P [AT58]). Ours: a caption page every ~1.1-1.5 s, a
   story event every ~1.5-3 s from the subject's own build, a join every >= 3.5 s on average (DIRECTION rule 1); never
   an interrupt for its own sake.
10. **Past the hook, engagement is emotional.** Surprise concentrates attention and joy keeps viewers from clicking
    away (eye tracking and face reading in 250 ms steps on 28 ads [AT47]); content that evokes awe, a high-arousal
    positive feeling, is shared more, and sad content less [AT48]; curiosity buys attention and memory for the answer
    [AT60]. Our hook (the expected thing, then reality overtaking it) is the surprise; the warm world holds; the one
    scale moment is the awe.
11. **The owner's 41 ideas, scored for attention** (§7): the strongest (5 of 5) are the lit opening with the figure in
    it (C1 with E2), the figure where the lines meet (E1), the pattern with one break (D7) and the camera holding while
    the subject travels the line (E9): each stacks two or more proven levers on one point. Nine more score 4. The
    weakest (1 of 5) are the placement grids (A1, A3, A4, A5), balance (D3), parallel lines (B5) and a near foreground
    (C2): no evidence that they steer the eye, or evidence that they split it. They keep their place in the library as
    proportion and calm, never as the device that makes the eye land.

**The fixation budget (est.).** At ~3-4 fixations a second, a 1.7 s look holds ~5-7 fixations. Captions take ~30 % of
viewing time in subtitled film [AT21], so the picture gets ~3-5, and the first goes to the centre or to the onset. That
is room for ONE subject: one fixation to land on it, one or two to see what it does, one more after reading the
caption. Every second call spends one of these few fixations; this is the eye-movement form of DIRECTION rule 2's "one
place to look" and its <= 6 named objects.

**The preview from the words, and crowding.** While the eye reads the caption, the subject sits 4-6 degrees below it,
in the periphery. An object is recognised there only if its neighbours keep a critical spacing of about half the
eccentricity (Bouma's law, reviewed in [AT25]): at 4-6 degrees that is 2-3 degrees, **~150-220 px**. Closer neighbours
crowd the subject into a blur of features, the saccade down is aimed at "something" rather than at it, and it lands
later. A clear ring around the subject keeps it legible in the preview. The same law is useful the other way: small
things packed closer than their critical spacing merge into one texture, which is what tier C's tone-on-tone life should
do.

|---|---|---|
| 2 degrees | 148 | ~74 px |
| 4 degrees (the short drop) | 296 | ~148 px |
| 6 degrees (the long drop) | 444 | ~222 px |
| 8 degrees (650 px, the far limit of R7) | 592 | ~296 px |

**What modern film teaches about attention (ours).** The trend the owner calls "super modern cinematography" is,
measured, more control of the eye: shorter shots, more motion, darker frames [AT45], gaze tightly synchronised across
viewers whether or not they understood [AT44], cuts hidden on motion [AT42]. Its tools are the levers above: one point of
motion, the subject at the centre of the shot, contrast and light on it, the next shot's subject where the eye already
is. For a 30-45 s explainer the modern pace is in the EVENTS (a caption page every ~1.2 s, a story change every ~2 s),
not in the cuts: our shots stay >= 3.5 s on average, each carrying several events (L15).

**Weak or unproven as gaze guides** (they keep their place as proportion and calm):
- **The rule of thirds:** no preference for it was found in large sets of photographs and paintings (`cinema.md` [S3]);
  in one eye-tracking study it made images more interesting to experts, not to novices [AT31]. In our frame the thirds'
  verticals sit at the edges of the subject axis, and the centre wins the first look anyway [S7], [V5].
- **The golden ratio and the golden spiral:** Fechner-style preference is "at best, inconclusive" ([S4]); our phi grid
  works because it lands near the centre (L4), not because of phi.
- **The eye travelling a line or a curve:** it does not; it jumps between key elements, and a composition's lines show
  up only as repeated saccades between them [AT31]. A line helps by ending at the subject (a convergence), not as a path.
- **Balance as such:** two equal masses are two equal calls (L6).

**The pen tip is a target the eye can ride (ours).** The eye follows one moving point smoothly up to about 30 degrees
a second; faster, it falls back on catch-up saccades [AT61]. On the owner's feed that is ~2,200 px/s, ~37 px a frame at
60 fps. A draw-on whose ONE leading pen moves at ~12-25 px a frame (10-20 degrees/s, est.) can be followed, so the
viewer reads the subject's outline while it forms: the reels' own device, "the line first, then the world under it"
(`reels.md` §5), works for exactly this reason. Several pens at once, or one faster than ~37 px a frame, break that into
jumps: keep one pen leading on the subject; supporting lines may draw at the same time only in low contrast.

**Never in the window:** a title over the captions (text against text, and the playbook's rule); a flash or a
full-frame colour change (an onset with no place to look); the camera and the subject moving at once (two motions:
COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 12's pivot); several things popping at the same moment; a pale or thin first effect (a subtle
effect counts as none: playbook Hook and pacing).

## 5. Holding attention in short form: hooks, pattern interrupts, change rate, clutter

### 5.1 What the platforms and the industry measure (mostly P and 1)

- **TikTok:** "Introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds", "Prioritize your hook in the first 6
  seconds" [V2]; "over 63% of all videos with the highest click-through rate (CTR) highlight their key message or
  product within the first 3 seconds" [AT51] (a correlation over its top auction ads, from a 2021 study by an attention
  vendor whose sample is not published: grade 1).
- **Meta:** up to 47 % of a video campaign's value came in its first 3 s and 74 % in its first 10 s (Nielsen for
  Facebook, 2016; reports read "value" as recall or lift) [AT52]; captions raised view time by 12 % in Facebook's
  internal tests, and 41 % of video ads were "basically meaningless without sound" [AT53]; 1.7 s per mobile feed item
  [V4].
- **YouTube:** ads that follow its ABCDs (attention, branding, connection, direction) showed +30 % short-term sales
  likelihood and +17 % long-term brand contribution in Kantar's scoring of 11,000 ads [AT54]; for Shorts, "be
  unavoidable", "tight framing", "announce yourself with audio" [V1]; its Shorts report gives the share of viewers who
  stayed past the first seconds (no published cutoff; creators aim for 70-80 %: P) [AT57].
- **Independent data:** in 9.2 million donated TikTok views (347 users) the median view covered 82 % of a video's
  length, yet most users watched only 20-60 % of their videos to the end [AT56]; an attention vendor measures ~1.5-2 s
  of active attention on fast-decay feeds and still finds outcomes from ~1.5 s when the brand's distinctive assets show
  [AT55].
- **Ours** (playbook Performance log, Hook and pacing): v1 kept 76 % of viewers at 2 s, v3 40 %; a calm, pretty
  opening lost ~80 % by 3 s; viewers left once the story felt resolved, ~40 s.

### 5.2 The hook frame: what the evidence asks of it

One subject on the axis and big (L4, L9); the frame's only onset on it (L1) with a sound (L10); the hardest contrast and
the brightest area on it (L5); a calm, uniform field around it (L6); the first spoken line in the band (L2); a person
only if the story has one, turned toward the subject (L3, L11); a gist that reads from the masses alone, at a squint
(R1; [AT1], [AT3]); and a tension the viewer can predict: the expected thing staged so the eye forms an expectation,
broken at ~2 s by reality (surprise [AT47]; curiosity [AT60]). This is the playbook's hook rule with its mechanism:
nothing in it is new to our docs, but each part now has its reason and its number.

### 5.3 Pattern interrupts

- **What the science calls them:** orienting responses. Cuts, motion onsets and text onsets each trigger an involuntary
  orienting response (Lang 1990; Thorson and Lang 1992, via [AT46]); camera changes inside one scene raised arousal and
  memory, from slow to very fast rates, without much extra load [AT46] (2000); paired with arousing content, a fast
  pace overloads viewers and memory drops [AT46] (1999).
- **What practitioners say:** "a visual change every ~2-4 s" or "every 3-5 s" (P [AT58]: several creator and tool
  guides, none with a published method; one warns that changes forced on a timer do not work, another that a jump cut
  does not always refresh attention).
- **What counts as one for us:** a change in the STORY's state on the subject: the next step of the mechanism, a carrier
  move, a light change on the subject, the scale reveal, a "But" turn. Each one persistent: it leaves its new state on
  screen for >= 0.5-1 s, so a viewer who was reading the caption catches it on the way back.
- **What does not count:** a decorative zoom, a whoosh with nothing changing, a random cut, a shake. They spend an
  orienting response on nothing, and DIRECTION rule 8 forbids them anyway (no zoom in a hold, no roll, no compound move).
- **Peaks and valleys:** alternating emotional peaks held viewers better than a sustained high [AT47]: hook (surprise),
  the mechanism (calm), the turn (surprise), the scale moment (awe), the payoff (joy).

### 5.4 Change rate: the numbers we use (ours)

| unit | rate | why |
|---|---|---|
| a caption page | ~1.1-1.5 s (<= 20 characters a second: V6) | the reading pace; each page is an onset in the band |
| a story event on the subject | every ~1.5-3 s | the practitioners' 2-5 s, tighter because our words move on every ~1.2 s and the picture must keep up with them (temporal contiguity, L7) |
| a join (a new panel) | >= 3.5 s on average (DIRECTION rule 1), 10-12 in a 40 s film | each join resets the eye to the centre (L4) and costs a search; related, carried joins are the kind that raised memory [AT46] |
| transient events the viewer must catch (a flash of light, a tick, a gesture that reverts) | >= 0.5 s apart | a second target within ~0.5 s of the first is often missed: the attentional blink, recovered by ~0.5-0.7 s [AT40] |
| the opening's build | a new piece every 3-6 frames (`CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md`), as ONE growing build | one tracked object, not many targets: the blink does not apply while the pieces belong to one thing |

### 5.5 The cost of clutter (what it costs, in numbers)

- Feature complexity (dense edges, colours and luminance variation) lowered attention to the key element and the liking
  of 249 ads; design complexity (more objects, more creative arrangement) raised attention and liking [AT26].
- Clutter (feature congestion) predicts search time [AT24]; the emotion on a smaller face is categorised more slowly,
  more so in clutter, and popular films hold such shots longer [AT28].
- Crowding: neighbours within ~half the eccentricity make an object unrecognisable in the periphery (§2's table: ~150 px
  at the short drop, ~220 px at the long one) [AT25].
- So the busy v6 frame (10 value calls, 61 % quiet: `reels.md` §6) costs fixations, not just beauty: its fourth and
  fifth calls each take one of the ~3-5 fixations the picture gets in a 1.7 s look (§2). The fix is the one DIRECTION
  rules 2-3 already give: the many things as texture inside masses, few named objects, two quiet fields.

  the L- and C-shapes, the S-curve, the foreground hint, unbalanced, the phi grid) are calm or proportion devices that
  need the light and the onset to carry the eye.
- **For the library (a proposal for the distill):** a beat's key moment uses a 4-5 device (or stacks a 2-3 device with
  the light, the onset and the singleton); calm beats, breathers and transitions may use a 2-3 device alone.

## 8. How animations and transitions fit (ours)

Every move is an attention event or a quiet one. The evidence sorts our moves by what they do to the eye; the right
column is where each belongs.

| move | what it does to the eye | where it belongs | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **draw-on** (one pen) | a moving point the eye can follow up to ~30 degrees/s (~37 px a frame on the feed); the outline is read as it forms | frame 0's subject; later only where the drawing is the beat (DIRECTION rule 8) | [AT61]; [S9]; `reels.md` §5 |
| **pop / grow** (looming) | captures | tier A's arrivals and the thread object's; never B or C | [AT11], [AT10] |
| **slide in** (translating) | captures | A, and the carrier D; B only from an edge at a constant speed, far from the focal | [AT11] |
| **wipe** (a moving edge) | an onset that runs along a line and leaves the eye where it stops | a carrier D: its leading edge ends at the next focal (est.) | [AT10]; est. |
| **shrink, slide out, recede** | weak capture | exits, for every tier | [AT11] |
| **colour flood, a light coming on** | a luminance onset at its place | the hook's flood; a lamp switching on with the action | [AT6], [AT36], [AT37] |
| **caption pop** | a text onset: it pulls the eye to the band | the band only; the picture's event 0.1-0.3 s before it | [AT16], [AT46], [AT34] |
| **parallax float** | continuous, slow motion: no capture | every hold (`lib/depth.ts`) | [AT10] |
| **camera push-in** | the whole field expands around the target; the target, still in screen, is the singleton among moving planes | a story push or carrier A; the target holds its screen spot within +-40 px (COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 12) | [S6]; est. |
| **plain cut (F)** | the eye resets toward the centre and searches | "But" turns only, the new focal on the centre | [AT15] |
| **match on action, carriers A-E** | the cut hides in the motion: a third of continuity cuts on a motion onset went unseen | every join | [AT42], [AT41] |
| **dissolve** | two half-contrast pictures and no onset: two weak calls at once | never as a join (DIRECTION rule 1; a declared time-passing exception only) | [AT22] |
| **flash, shake, whip pan** | a full-frame onset with no place to look | never | est.; DIRECTION rule 8 |

**Three rules for motion inside a shot (ours):**
1. **One motion leads at a time.** At an onset, either the camera moves or the subject moves; the still subject among
   moving planes and the moving subject in a still frame both make a motion singleton; both at once make two motions
   (COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 12's pivot, E3, E9).
2. **Animation earns its place by showing the mechanism.** Animations beat static pictures for learning by d ≈ 0.37
   overall and d ≈ 0.40 when they represent the process itself rather than decorate it [AT38]: the hook's "real
   mechanism" (`CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md`) is the kind that helps; a decorative move is a seductive detail (L8).
3. **The join is the most expensive moment for the eye.** It resets the gaze toward the centre and starts a search
   (L4); carried on the subject's motion and landing within ~220 px of where the eye already is, it costs almost
   nothing (AR13). That is the attention case for DIRECTION rule 1's "every join carries something".

## 9. Clashes and refinements (flagged for the orchestrator; not edited here)

1. **`CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` "The opening" ("a new element every 3-6 frames").** Right for ONE growing build (one tracked
   object); a clash when the elements are separate things to read (labels, numbers, icons): transient events under
   ~0.5 s apart are often missed [AT40]. **Proposal:** add "as one build; separate reads >= 0.5 s apart" (AR6).
2. **DIRECTION rule 8 ("visuals lead their word by ~0.1 s").** The evidence for words with the picture (d ≈ 1.2-1.3
   [AT34]) supports a lead of 0.1 s to ~0.3 s; a longer lead splits the look. **Proposal:** "by 0.1-0.3 s, never after
   the word". A range, not a clash.
3. **DIRECTION rule 3 and `LIFE_AND_DENSITY.md` ("Life never stops (clouds, steam, snow, birds, a window lighting,
   walkers)").** A window lighting is a luminance onset, and birds and walkers are animate motion: both capture
   [AT10], [AT14]. `CRAFT.md` §b's turn-taking (~0.6 entries a second, none in the 0.4 s around a join) covers part of
   it. **Proposal:** add "a light-up or an entry of supporting life >= 370 px from the focal and outside the subject's
   onset +-0.5 s; birds fly straight and evenly" (AR5, AR8).
4. **`reels.md` E3 ("the subject is still while life passes sideways in one band behind it").** It holds only when the
   passing life is an even flow; one mover passing behind the subject pulls the eye off it [AT13]. **Proposal** for the
   library's recipe: "a flow, never a single mover".
5. **DIRECTION rule 7 and COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 7 (the "you" at 130-220 px near the camera).** Bodies capture attention
   [AT18], so the "you" is a second magnet, most of all when the subject is an object (a window, a machine). The
   safeguards in COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 7 (tier B, its pull < 50 % of the focal's, its sightline crossing the focal's
   box) are right; **proposal:** make them checks, not notes.
6. **COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 13 and V9 ("read as one gist at 0.25 s").** Supported: the gist comes from 19-67 ms of
   looking [AT1], the first saccade ~0.2 s after the page settles. **Proposal:** add the second change on the subject at
   ~1.2-1.7 s (AR15).
7. **The playbook's "words in the picture are few and big".** Strengthened: even unreadable, upside-down or
   unknown-script text pulls the eye [AT17]. **Proposal:** "no rows of glyph-like marks outside the band" in
   `LAYOUT.md` §3 (AR7).
8. **DIRECTION rule 2's Pip ("found on a second look, never on the subject's eye path").** Consistent with the evidence
   on seductive details [AT35] and animate motion [AT14]; **refinement:** Pip never moves with animate starts or turns
   within 370 px of the focal.
9. **Not a clash, a missing number:** no doc says how far apart two calls must sit to stay two calls rather than one
   texture. Crowding gives one (§2: ~0.5 x the distance from where the eye rests). **Proposal:** use it in R3's call
   count (`organic/composition.md`) as the merge distance for small pieces.
