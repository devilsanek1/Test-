## 0. Bottom line

1. **Modern practice adds almost no new geometry to the owner's 41 ideas; what changed is how pictures are watched.**
   A phone, several crops of one file, the swipe, faster cutting, stylised animation. These conditions favour a handful
   of the owner's ideas, done bolder and simpler: one centred subject, a big quiet field, one bold colour on the
   subject, light that makes the focal, depth stacked up the frame, and joins that keep the eye where it already is
   (§2).
2. **What captures the eye, by evidence** (§1): the onset of motion and looming (STUDY); text (11x the looks of a
   control region) and faces (17x) (STUDY); the hardest value step (our measurement of the reels); a lone colour
   (STUDY, its "capture" contested); the screen centre for the first look (STUDY). **What holds it:** pictures that are
   easy to process, figure clear of ground (STUDY review); designed arrangement without pixel clutter (STUDY: feature
   complexity lowers attention and liking, design complexity raises both); one highlight at a time (META: retention g
   = 0.53); nothing interesting-but-irrelevant (META: seductive details hurt); cuts between related shots (STUDY).
3. **Centre-weighted framing is the vertical default for three measured reasons** (M1): the first look goes to the
   centre; the centre is the one region every crop keeps (the owner's feed, the 4:5 and 3:4 grid crops, TikTok, the ad
   zones); and a subject that stays near the centre needs no search after a join. The talk of a "trend" toward centring
   is anecdotal; these reasons are not.
4. **The join is where modern practice and the evidence agree most** (M12, M19-M23). Cuts between related shots leave
   better memory than unrelated ones, which cost more processing (STUDY); continuity cuts were missed 32 % of the time
   against 9 % for cuts to unrelated scenes (STUDY); continuity and repeated colour across a cut speed the eye's
   re-orientation (STUDY); sound carries much of the effect (STUDY). For our frame: the attended thing within **150 px
   (2 degrees) of its last spot** across a join, 370 px (5 degrees) at most, in the same colour, the cut on the next
   sentence's first word. DIRECTION rule 1's "every join carries something" is the evidence-backed choice, not only a
   style.
5. **The continuous camera is the modern prestige move** (M13): one-take TV episodes (2025), an Oscar-winning animated
   feature made of long exploratory takes (2024), one-take FPV fly-through ads as a genre (2020-2024), short-form's
   "seamless transitions". Our C and A carriers are this grammar; video 4's one continuous camera is in step with it.
6. **In 9:16, stack in depth and move along Z and Y** (M5, M18): vertical-native makers push and pull rather than truck,
   and stage people near-low and far-high rather than side by side (CONSENSUS); the tall frame exaggerates sideways
   motion (ONE SOURCE). A push is looming, the motion that captures attention; receding motion does not (STUDY, M14).
7. **Modern stylised animation replaced optical effects with drawn ones** (M10, M16): depth from colour offset or from
   broader brushwork instead of blur, the painterly steps composed by the artists, characters stepped on twos while the
   camera glides on ones (ONE SOURCE each, across four features). All of it is already in DIRECTION rules 4, 5 and 8:
   our direction is current, not dated.
8. **The draw-on is backed, with one nuance** (M27): watching an instructor's hand draw improved transfer (d = 0.35),
   while the same drawing appearing with no hand in view did not (d = -0.16) (STUDY, from summaries); whiteboard
   animation raised engagement and retention (STUDY, from summaries). Keep the playbook's visible moving pen tip, bold,
   in every draw-on.
9. **Light and colour, the modern version** (M8, M9): lit practicals in the frame as the story light, warm against one
   cool field, one saturated accent on the subject; never the flat, murky "streaming look" critics took apart in
   2022-2023, never two-colour neon as a default.
10. **Negative space, and the rule broken on purpose** (M2-M4): negative space gives the one thing its weight
    (prestige TV, ONE SOURCE); our sky with the captions already is that space (60-68 % of the frame in setup A).
    Short-siding, the low-quadrant frame and broken symmetry are modern tools for unease: we keep them for the "But"
    turn, once a film.
11. **Top of the ranking (§5):** M1 centre-weighted framing, M19 eye trace across the join, M12 related cuts, M25
    coherence and M26 signalling score 15 of 15; then M11 graphic simplicity, M27 the live drawing, M2 negative space,
    M5 stacking in depth and M8 one bold colour (14). At the bottom, true but small: M16 stepped drawings and M18
    sideways speed (9).
12. **What we don't take** (§7): shallow focus and blur, handheld shake, roll and Dutch angles, whip pans with blur,
    crash-zoom tics, glitch and RGB-split joins, the flat streaming look, two-colour neon light, isometric world views,
    split screens, loud grain and paper edges, titles over the picture.

## 1. What captures attention, and what holds it (the levers, ranked by evidence)

The owner asks "what makes a composition to be engaging and capturing attention". These are two things: **capture**
(where the eye goes in the first 0.1-0.5 s of a frame or after a change) and **holding** (whether the viewer keeps
watching and understands). The levers, the strongest evidence first:

| capture lever | what the evidence says | strength | its cost to our calm look | ours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **motion onset** | the abrupt start of motion captures attention [N11]; in film, flicker and motion are the strongest predictors of where viewers look, and the moments when everyone looks at one place are predicted by motion [N8] | STUDY | high when spread: every onset pulls | one onset at a time, on the subject or the thread; supporting movers take turns and never start in the 0.4 s round a join (DIRECTION 3) |
| **looming** (growing, approaching) | translating and looming stimuli capture attention, receding ones do not [N11] | STUDY (debated in detail: onset or motion itself) | low: one push | a push-in is an attention event (M14); a pull-out needs its own event to be seen |
| **text** | free viewers fixate text 11.1x and faces 16.6x more than control regions of the same size and place, and find them hard to avoid even at a cost [N14]; in print ads, text draws attention in proportion to its surface, while the picture captures attention whatever its size [N17] | STUDY | high: every word competes with the subject | words only in the band (M29); one label or one number in the world at most |
| **faces and people** | as above; in moving pictures the eye defaults to the centre, to motion and to people [cinema S8] | STUDY | we draw no faces; silhouettes still pull | the "you" soft (tier B), from behind; strangers small and in groups |
| **the hardest value step** | in 12 of the 20 one-subject reel examples a brighter patch elsewhere beat the subject the lines pointed at (`reels.md` §0) | our measurement | none: it is the plan | the story light on the subject (R4; playbook PROPER LIGHT) |
| **a lone colour** | a colour singleton pops out of a field; whether it captures attention against the viewer's goal depends on the search mode [N13] | STUDY (contested) | low if there is one | one hot accent at the focal (M8) |
| **the centre** | the first look at a new scene goes to the centre whatever it holds [cinema S7]; in vertical video a centred subject gave longer fixations and fewer saccades [V5] | STUDY | none | the focal home (M1) |

| holding lever | what the evidence says | strength | ours |
|---|---|---|---|
| **fluency** | figure-ground contrast, symmetry, good form and repetition make a picture easier to process, and the ease is felt as beauty; too simple or too expected loses it [N15] | STUDY (review) | one closed subject shape on a calm field (R1, R4); M4 |
| **designed, not cluttered** | feature complexity (dense colours, edges, luminance variation) lowered attention to the brand and the attitude to the ad; design complexity (more objects, irregular shapes, an elaborate arrangement) raised attention to the ad and liking [N16] | STUDY | full but never busy (DIRECTION 3): M11 |
| **coherence** | interesting but irrelevant details hurt recall and transfer, most when they are pictures and when study time is limited, as in a video that sets its own pace [N18] | META | M25 |
| **signalling** | cues that highlight the essential raised retention (g = 0.53) and transfer (g = 0.33) across 145 comparisons [N19] | META | M26 |
| **related cuts, continuity** | related and unrelated cuts both trigger an orienting response; unrelated ones cost more processing and leave weaker memory, visual memory most [N3]; continuity cuts go unnoticed [N6] | STUDY | M12, M19 |
| **the live drawing** | the instructor's hand drawing raised transfer; whiteboard animation raised engagement and retention [N20, N21] | STUDY | M27 |
| **pacing** | faster pacing raises the resources viewers spend; fast pacing with arousing content overloads them and recall drops [N4] | STUDY (summary) | new things often, inside calm content (M12) |

**The rule this gives us** (est., from the two tables): capture with ONE lever at a time, at the subject (an onset or a
push, the hardest value step, the hot accent and the caption's keyword, all on the same word); hold with fluency,
coherence and continuity. A frame that pulls with several levers in several places splits the eye: in attention terms,
that is the owner's "our scene is very busy".

## 3. The modern principles (ours: M1-M29)

Each principle: **what** it is; **why it works now**, with the evidence and its strength; **in current work**, what
makers do with it (described, never named as a target); **ours**, the verdict and the numbers for our frame; and the
owner's ideas it extends. Five groups: A framing, B light and colour, C time and camera, D joins, E explainer
composition.

### A. Framing: where things sit

**M1 Centre-weighted framing: the crop-safe axis.** YES (it is our subject axis; the reasons and the crop box are new).
- *What:* the subject on or near the frame's vertical centre line, and the frame built so the story survives every crop
  the platforms make of one file.
- *Why now:* (1) the first look at a new scene goes to the centre [cinema S7], and on phones a centred subject in
  vertical video gave longer fixations and fewer saccades than a moving one [V5] (STUDY); (2) one file is shown in many
  crops: the owner's Reels tab x 98-982, a profile x 52-1028, the grid's 4:5 crop (y 285-1635) and 3:4 crop (y
  240-1680), TikTok's full width, the ad templates (`vertical.md` §1, `../LAYOUT.md` §1; measured and official): the
  centre is the only region all of them keep; (3) a subject near the centre in every shot lands where the eye already
  is after each join (M19). The talk of a centring "trend" in film and on Instagram is opinion (ONE SOURCE each [N38]);
  a qualitative study of vertical short films reports centred compositions and mostly medium shots to close-ups (ONE
  SOURCE, from a summary [N40]).
- *In current work:* vertical makers' guides tell you to centre the subject and to compose for 9:16 from the start
  rather than crop a landscape frame [N38].
- *Ours:* the focal's centre within 130 px of x 540 (x 410-670) and at y 860-1200 by default (R7;
  `../COMPOSITION_SHOTS.md` rule 3); every key read inside the **crop-safe stage, x 130-870 by y 600-1519** (est.:
  inside every organic view measured, and inside the cover's 4:5 crop); for a boosted post, above y 1247 and left of x
  780 below y 840 (`vertical.md` V14). Off the centre only for a declared reason (a scale shot's landmark, the
  left-third hero spot, the turn: M3). Extends A1, A2, B2.

**M2 Negative space that gives the one thing its weight.** YES.
- *What:* a large calm field with one small subject in it; the emptiness is the composition.
- *Why now:* on a small screen one subject in a quiet field reads in one glance (fluency [N15], STUDY review); a
  cinematographer of a 2022-2025 office drama describes working out what was interesting in a set with almost nothing in
  it, and using negative space to give weight to particular objects; its photography is described as balancing subjects
  with still life and negative space (ONE SOURCE, from summaries [N35]); the owner's painted models are 67-91 % quiet at
  a squint (our measurement: R5, `../organic/composition.md`).
- *In current work:* near-empty production design (a field of identical desks, a blank corridor) with one figure in it;
  product films with one object on one colour.
- *Ours:* the sky with the caption band IS our negative space: 60-68 % of the frame in setup A, 70-80 % on a breather
  (SR16). The subject is small in it (a 180 px figure covers about 0.5 % of the frame, a 150 x 230 px lit window about
  1.7 %: est.) and sits at the field's foot; the field is patterned within +-4-6 L* so it is never a hole (DIRECTION
  3); nothing but the subject and the words calls in it. The owner's "big quiet areas and one place to look" is this
  principle. Extends D1, A6, E1, D7.

**M3 Short-siding and the low quadrant: the rule broken for unease.** ADAPT (once a film, on the turn).
- *What:* the subject pushed to the frame's edge looking out of it (no lead room), or set low in a corner under a heavy
  weight of empty frame.
- *Why now:* viewers prefer a side-facing subject to face into the frame [cinema S10] (STUDY), so breaking it is felt; a
  2015-2019 TV drama made the low-quadrant frame its signature, its makers setting out "to break as many rules of image
  composition as they could", with characters "beneath an oppressive amount of headroom" (ONE SOURCE, from summaries
  [N36]); widely imitated in the 2020s.
- *Ours:* only on the "But" turn (SR18), at most once a film, never in a cozy beat. Short-sided: the subject at x
  130-300 facing LEFT, out of the frame (the right side belongs to the rail), its feet above y 1480, the empty stage
  behind it. Low quadrant: the figure's head below y 1150 with >= 60 % of the frame above it as one quiet field. The
  next beat returns to the focal home. Extends B10, D4, E4.

**M4 Formal symmetry, then the break.** ADAPT (once or twice a film).
- *What:* a strictly mirrored frame for order, an institution, a ritual or a stillness; then one element breaks the
  symmetry on the story's turn.
- *Why now:* symmetry is among the variables that make a picture fluent and liked [N15] (STUDY review); analyses of a
  2022-2025 drama read its symmetric frames as the company's control and their break as disruption (ONE SOURCE,
  secondary [N35]).
- *Ours:* the axis x 540 (the caption's centre line, x 520, is 20 px off it); the two halves within +-5 % in mass; at
  most two such frames a film (cinema C14; SR23). The break is ONE change at the axis on the turn's word: the story
  light or the thread moves off the axis, one window lights on one side, or the next shot's subject lands off the axis
  (SR18, SR19). Extends A7.

**M5 Stack in depth, move along Z: the vertical's staging.** YES.
- *What:* in a tall frame, the near subject low and the far one higher, at different depths, never side by side; the
  camera moves toward and away (push, pull, rise, crane) and up and down (tilt) more than sideways.
- *Why now:* the owner's feed shows 884 px across and the full 1920 up: height is the frame's resource (`vertical.md`
  §2); vertical makers' guides advise depth moves in place of lateral dollies, subjects moving toward or away from the
  camera "to create more depth", and story information placed on different vertical planes (CONSENSUS, from makers' and
  vendors' guides [N38]); vertical micro-dramas, the big new vertical genre, stack two people rather than line them up,
  because side-by-side lineups look awkward (CONSENSUS, weaker sources [N39]); a looming move captures attention (M14),
  and motion parallax alone conveys depth (STUDY [N12]).
- *Ours:* the "you" near and low (130-220 px, feet at y ~1220-1510: SR14), the subject farther and higher on the axis
  (centre y 950-1200); two figures never side by side at one depth: the far one 1/2.2-1/3 of the near one's height
  (Scale cues 2), their tops >= 40 px apart, overlapping >= 10 px or >= 20 px apart (C13). Camera moves in order of
  preference: push or pull (Z), rise or tilt (Y), truck (X) only when the travel is the story (SR10). Extends B1, C3,
  E10.

**M6 The look-through: foreground occlusion, drawn.** ADAPT.
- *What:* a near object partly covers the frame (a door jamb, a branch, a window bar, a railing), so the viewer looks
  through it at the subject: depth, and the feeling of a hidden observer.
- *Why now:* occlusion (one thing in front of another) is a basic depth cue in every account of layout perception
  (CONSENSUS; the classic review sorts the cues by distance [N12]); shooting through doorways and foliage is everywhere
  in prestige TV, and animated features paint near layers that pass the lens (S5's chimney pot is one).
- *Ours:* the owner's rule wins: "a small, subtle hint at the edges" (at most 2 props, cut by an edge, inside the near
  shade mass within 8 L*, one side, <= 216 px wide); never blurred (DIRECTION 5); never over the band or the focal; in a
  move a near mass may pass the lens as a wipe (M22). Extends C1, C2, E5.

**M7 Height as meaning, in modern use.** ADAPT.
- *What:* eye level for empathy, low for awe, high and top-down for systems and patterns, first person for "you".
- *Why now:* drones made high and top-down views cheap and common; a 2025 animated feature credits anime for its forced
  perspective and fluid camera in action (ONE SOURCE [N29]); first-person framing with hands in frame is native to the
  feed (our observation, est.); what each angle says is `cinema.md` §3.
- *Ours:* `cinema.md` C5 holds: eye level by default; a low angle on the quantity, never on the thing under critique; a
  high angle once a film, on crowds and patterns; overhead only as paper (a chart or a map lying in the place) or as the
  "you"'s own look down at its hands (SR13); no forced three-point perspective (`../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` "Not for us").
  Extends B6, B12, D2, E1, E11.

### B. Light, colour and rendering

**M8 One bold colour, on the subject.** YES.
- *What:* a restrained palette (tonal families, low chroma) and one saturated accent on the story's object, often
  recurring as the film's motif.
- *Why now:* a lone colour pops out of a field (STUDY [N13]; whether it captures attention against the viewer's goal is
  contested); in the owner's reels the hiker's blue shirt was the strongest call in value and colour (`reels.md` E8),
  and in still v6 four yellow upper windows were the strongest colour call, beating the lit door
  (`../organic/composition.md` §3): the lone accent works for whatever it sits on, right or wrong.
- *In current work:* colour blocking in production design; one brand colour in ads; music-video gels (we take the single
  accent, not the two-colour neon: M9).
- *Ours:* one hot accent per scene, at the focal (DIRECTION 6): its chroma about twice tier B's cap (C* >= 50 against
  B's <= 25: est.), its area within ~3-5 % of the frame (est.), every other colour tone-on-tone in its mass (dE <= 6-8:
  COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 4); the accent travels with the thread object across the joins (M20); never a second saturated
  patch on the stage. Extends E8.

**M9 The practical as the story light: motivated, stylised light.** YES for one colour key; NO to two-colour neon.
- *What:* the light comes from sources in the frame (a lamp, a lit door, a window, a screen), shaped and coloured for
  the mood.
- *Why now:* the brightest area decides where the eye lands (`reels.md` §0); a 2025 animated feature lit its
  performances like concerts and its quiet moments like music videos, with strong gel lights and colourful rims (ONE
  SOURCE [N29]); a 2022 animated feature's VFX supervisor describes the light compositing abstracting detail toward a
  painterly look, each step reviewed with the production designer for composition (ONE SOURCE, from summaries [N30]);
  a 2023 series built on woodblock-print principles concentrated its contrast and kept heavy volumetric light off its
  characters to keep a 2D feel (ONE SOURCE [N33]).
- *Ours:* playbook PROPER LIGHT already: one story light per scene, on the subject; a practical in the frame whenever
  the shot allows (the lit door, the one window, the nook's lamp); the brightest 5 % below the sky within 134-288 px of
  the subject (COMPOSITION_SHOTS rule 4); its glow and bloom at the source only, ending >= 10 px outside the band
  (SR6); rims only in the key's colour family; warm practicals against one cool field (DIRECTION 6). NO: complementary
  two-colour neon as a default lighting scheme (it breaks the one colour key); volumetric shafts as a show
  (`../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` "Not for us"). Extends E2.

**M10 Drawn depth instead of optical blur.** YES (already ours).
- *What:* stylised features separate near from far with drawn cues instead of a lens's blur: colour misregistration,
  broader and fewer brush marks, fewer values, the hue of the air.
- *Why now:* blur reads photographic and turns to mush on a small screen; a 2018 feature replaced depth-of-field blur
  with a printing-style colour offset (secondhand: blog and forum accounts [N32]); a 2024 feature drew its environments
  as hand-painted strokes, thinking in "strokes, splatters, and brushstrokes" rather than filtering realistic models
  (ONE SOURCE, from summaries [N31]); a 2022 feature abstracted its detail through lighting and compositing (ONE SOURCE
  [N30]); a 2020 feature drew its rigid town and its free forest in two line languages, woodcut geometry against loose
  round strokes (ONE SOURCE [N34]).
- *Ours:* DIRECTION rules 4 and 5 already: the paint coarsens with distance, the near and the focal sharpest, no blur,
  no fog; the numbers are `../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` Scale cues 5 (one fixed mix toward the sky per plane, 0 / 12 / 22 /
  40 / 65 %, values 5 / 5 / 4 / 3 / 2). NO to the colour offset: it is a fringe that reads as an outline (DIRECTION 4),
  and a 2-3 px offset is smeared by the half-resolution colour of every platform encode (`vertical.md` [V15]). The
  forest-and-town contrast is the owner's ORGANIC, NOT GEOMETRIC seen from the other side: geometry reads as order and
  control, so our world stays organic. Extends E5, E6.

**M11 Design complexity up, feature complexity down.** YES.
- *What:* a rich arrangement (several designed shapes, an irregular organic layout, one clear device) with little
  pixel-level clutter (few competing edges, colours and value flickers).
- *Why now:* in eye-tracked ads, feature complexity (dense perceptual features: colour, edges, luminance) lowered
  attention to the brand and the attitude to the ad, while design complexity (the creative arrangement of objects and
  shapes) raised attention to the ad and liking (STUDY [N16]); fluency [N15]; seductive details (M25); 2025-2026
  motion-design roundups name minimal animation with organic textures and layered depth among the leading looks (ONE
  SOURCE each, blogs [N41]).
- *Ours:* the owner's "full but never busy" in numbers we already hold: the arrangement may be rich (one device, varied
  organic silhouettes, 4-6 planes, the far side's density as texture inside its band), while feature complexity stays
  low away from the focal: <= 10 value pieces at a squint (R2), the quiet fields within +-4-6 L*, far texture
  tone-on-tone (+-6 L*), found edges only at the focal (R9), detail peaking just under the horizon and nowhere else
  (Scale cues 4). Extends D7 and the playbook's SCENE COMPOSITION.

### C. Time and camera

**M12 The cut is an attention event: ration it, and make it related.** YES.
- *What:* film has cut ever faster for a century, and short-form faster still; but every cut costs the viewer something.
- *Why now:* shot lengths in English-language film fell steadily for a hundred years, about log-linearly, from ~12-13 s
  (1930s-1940s) to ~4 s (1985-2005) and lower since, with no jump at the music-video era (STUDY [N1, N2]); later films
  also carry more motion, the shortest shots the most (STUDY [N1]). Every cut triggers an orienting response; unrelated
  cuts (to a new scene) slowed viewers' responses (more processing) and left weaker memory, visual memory most, while
  related cuts (within a scene) left better memory (STUDY [N3]); fast pacing with arousing content overloads viewers and
  recall drops (STUDY, from summaries [N4]). TikTok tells advertisers to state the proposition in the first 3 s and to
  "utilize transitions" to hold engagement (ONE SOURCE, official [N25]).
- *Ours:* novelty often, cuts rarely. Something new every ~1-2 s from the subject's own build (the caption pages already
  change every 1.1-1.5 s: `vertical.md` §5); shots (panels) average >= 3.5 s (DIRECTION 1); every join related
  (carriers A-E); at most 2 plain cuts F, each on a "But" turn. Our content is calm, not arousing, so a quick pace of
  change inside a continuous shot sits on the safe side of the overload finding (est.). Extends E9.

**M13 The continuous camera: the long take and the seamless join.** YES.
- *What:* one camera moving through a space in place of cuts: a one-take episode, a long exploratory take, an FPV flight
  through doors and windows, a short-form "seamless transition".
- *Why now:* a 2025 drama shot each of its four episodes as one continuous take, for immersion and real time, planned on
  miniatures with a small model camera (ONE SOURCE, several reports [N27]); a 2024 animated feature, an Oscar winner,
  explored rough virtual sets with a virtual camera "almost like on a live-action film set" and is built on long takes
  (ONE SOURCE, from summaries [N26]); one-take FPV fly-throughs became an ad genre from 2020 (an 87 s single take
  through a bowling alley; a one-take city ad that needed 39 attempts) (ONE SOURCE each [N28]). Continuity cuts go
  unnoticed (STUDY [N6]); the still thing among moving planes wins the eye [cinema S6], and motion predicts where
  everyone looks (STUDY [N8]).
- *Ours:* our C carrier (continuous camera) and A carrier (push-through) are this grammar; the opening is one continuous
  shot (DIRECTION 9); video 4's visual script runs 12 shots on one continuous camera (`../COMPOSITION_SHOTS.md` intro).
  Plan it the way the one-take makers do, on a model first (our G3 animatic), with the start, middle and end frames of
  every move designed (C12), one speed budget (~15 px per frame at 60 fps for detailed layers: PIPELINE_V2) and the
  subject as the pivot (+-40 px). Extends B2, E3, E9.

**M14 Motivated moves: a push is looming; a pull needs its own event.** YES.
- *What:* every camera move has a story reason: the push says "this matters", the pull says "see where it sits", the
  reveal pays off a look.
- *Why now:* looming captures attention, receding does not (STUDY [N11]); in Arcane, pulls let a moment sit and pushes
  mean attention, approach or impact (`../research/cine_motion.md` §1 rule 3); the push into a sub-frame is a standard
  motion-design join (`../research/animatic_mograph.md` §3).
- *Ours:* a story push reads at >= 3 % in <= 2 s (`../COZY_AND_SCALE.md` 3b.5), stays <= 2x per panel and lands on its
  word, the picture ~0.1 s ahead of the voice; a pull-out reveal carries its own attention event (S5: the light burst
  before the landing, the landmark rising on the payoff word, birds crossing toward it); no zoom in a hold (DIRECTION
  8). A push may aim at a spiral's eye, a vanishing point or a closed vista (A4, B2, E7). Extends A4, B1, D2, E7.

**M15 Asymmetric easing: decelerate in, accelerate out, move on arcs.** YES.
- *What:* current motion systems bring things in with a fast start and a long settle, and send them out with a slow
  start and a fast exit; paths curve.
- *Why now:* Material Design 3's emphasized curves, as its published token sets give them: decelerate cubic-bezier(0.05,
  0.7, 0.1, 1.0) for what enters, accelerate cubic-bezier(0.3, 0.0, 0.8, 0.15) for what leaves, durations in tiers from
  50 ms to about 1 s (~400 ms for a large entrance) (ONE SOURCE, official, via third-party token sets [N24]); Arcane's
  measured vertical reveals start fast and settle (`../research/cine_motion.md` §1 rule 5); arcs are one of the classic
  animation principles [cinema S5] (CONSENSUS).
- *Ours:* things that grow, slide or arrive (captions excepted: they pop in place) use a decelerating curve over
  0.25-0.45 s (15-27 frames at 60 fps); exits accelerate over 0.15-0.25 s (9-15 frames) (est.); the thread object
  travels on an arc or an S (B8), never on a ruler line; camera story moves keep their own measured shapes (S5's curve).
  Position and scale only, never opacity: UI systems fade things in, we don't (DIRECTION 8). Extends B8.

**M16 Stepped drawings, smooth camera.** YES (already ours).
- *What:* characters and drawn effects held for 2-4 frames (on twos or threes) while the camera, the parallax and any
  simulation move on every frame.
- *Why now:* a 2018 feature animated its characters mainly on twos for a graphic, punchy look, while its camera moves
  were often on ones; its 2023 sequel set a character on twos against another on ones to tell them apart (ONE SOURCE,
  from summaries [N32]); a 2022 feature stepped its action on twos, threes and fours (ONE SOURCE [N30]).
- *Ours:* DIRECTION 8 ("drawn poses may step while the camera glides"). At 60 fps, "twos" at 24 fps is a hold of 5
  frames (83 ms) and "threes" 7-8 frames (est.); the camera, the parallax float, the light and the captions move on
  every frame. New to the reels (none of their ideas is about timing).

**M17 2.5D: layers that move by depth, inside their limits.** YES, with the limits as numbers.
- *What:* painted layers at depths, moved by one camera: the documentary "2.5D photo" since 2002, the automated "3D Ken
  Burns" since 2019, camera projection in visual effects.
- *Why now:* motion parallax alone conveys shape and depth (STUDY [N12]); practitioners agree the illusion holds only
  within a range: texture stretching and holes appear when the camera moves too far, and it breaks first close to the
  lens (CONSENSUS [N42]); single-image parallax needs the holes a move uncovers painted in (technical study [N42]).
- *Ours:* one camera, planes by 1/z (DIRECTION 8; `../LAYOUT.md` §8.2). **The hole rule** (est.): between two
  neighbouring planes a move uncovers a strip as wide as their relative shift, so the farther plane is painted at least
  that far behind the nearer one's edge, plus 40 px (the float's overscan: `../LAYOUT.md` §1). Z moves (push, pull)
  uncover the least; sideways moves uncover the most, so a truck's background is drawn as the view plus the travel
  (SR10). Every plane carries its own perspective (converging edges, foreshortening), so no plane reads as a flat
  cut-out; 4-6 planes, 5-7 on a vista (`../CRAFT.md` §f.1); the nearest plane breaks first, so a big near mass moves
  only inside its overscan. Not in the reels (the engine's own).

**M18 Sideways motion reads faster in 9:16.** ADAPT.
- *What:* in a tall frame a sideways move crosses the picture quickly; a vertical move has room.
- *Why now:* the guide of the first vertical film festival warns that the 9:16 frame's taller lines and narrower field
  of view "tends to exaggerate horizontal (left-right) movement" (ONE SOURCE, from a summary [N38]); arithmetic: the
  owner's feed shows 884 px across and 1920 up, so a mover at the same px per frame crosses the width 2.2x sooner than
  the height (est.).
- *Ours:* for the same felt calm, give sideways movers (a truck, a walker crossing, clouds) about half the px per frame
  of a vertical move (est.); life passing sideways stays slow and low in contrast (DIRECTION 3); travel by tilt or rise
  where the story allows (SR10's tilt). Extends B5.

### D. Joins: how the eye crosses from shot to shot

Our carriers are `../CRAFT.md` §a's (DIRECTION rule 1): A push-through, B thread match, C continuous camera, D object
carry or wipe, E card flip, F plain cut (at most 2, on a "But" turn); a dissolve is no carrier. M19-M24 are the
evidence and the numbers under them.

**M19 Eye trace: the attended thing keeps its screen spot across the join.** YES.
- *What:* across a cut or a transition, the thing the eye is on in the outgoing shot, or the thing the next shot is
  about, sits where the eye already is.
- *Why now:* the continuity rules work by cueing attention across the cut with motion, gaze, sound and pointing (STUDY
  and theory [N5]); match-action cuts were missed 32.4 % of the time against 9.4 % for cuts between unrelated scenes
  (STUDY [N6]); viewers re-orient faster to a scene's continuation when the visual continuity across the cut is high
  (STUDY [N9]); eye trace is one of the six criteria of an ideal cut in the most quoted editing book (CONSENSUS [N10]);
  in professionally edited film nearly everyone looks at the same place at the same time (STUDY [N7]).
- *Ours* (est., from the feed's angles: §9): the next shot's focal centre within **150 px (2 degrees)** of the last
  shot's focal centre lands in the eye's sharp centre, no search; within **370 px (5 degrees)**, one short saccade
  (~0.2 s); beyond that only where a carrier leads the eye there (a mover, the camera's own path) or on an F cut, where
  the jolt is the point. With the focal home (M1) as every shot's default this mostly holds for free. The Arcane
  lane's light-match recipe keeps the bright shape within 190 px (0.1 H: `../research/cine_light.md` T1). Extends E3 and
  every join.

**M20 The feature match: colour, light and shape carried across the join.** YES.
- *What:* the outgoing and incoming shots share a feature at the attended spot: the same colour, the same bright shape,
  the same silhouette.
- *Why now:* features repeated across a cut speed the first saccade to the scene's continuation, colour among them,
  though even a high similarity guides the eye rather than grabbing it (STUDY [N9]); repetition is processed fluently
  [N15]; Arcane's light-match cuts correlate 0.81-0.90 in their layout (`../research/cine_light.md` T1).
- *Ours:* the B carrier (thread match): the anchor's centre within 150 px (190 px at most), its size within 1.5x, the
  same hue family (the scene's hot accent on the thread object), ONE change across the join (temperature, scale or
  world); the cut on the anchor's fastest movement or on the next sentence's first word (M23). Extends D6, E8.

**M21 The portal: through an opening into the next shot.** YES.
- *What:* the camera pushes into a door, a window, a screen or a round opening, and the opening becomes the next shot's
  frame; in interfaces, a container that grows into the next view.
- *Why now:* Material's container transform keeps one container on screen while its size, position and shape change into
  the next view, to show how the two relate (ONE SOURCE, official [N24]); the FPV fly-through's signature is passing
  through openings [N28]; the "infinite zoom" join is in the motion-design catalogue
  (`../research/animatic_mograph.md` §3).
- *Ours:* the A carrier (K9): the opening 15-25 % of the frame at the start, on the axis, its aperture the lightest area
  below the sky; a push 0 -> 0.85 in 1.2-2 s; the aperture steps up in light as it nears (Arcane T7); the next shot's
  focal where the aperture's centre was (M19). Extends B4, B13, C1.

**M22 The wipe by a near mover.** ADAPT (once or twice a film).
- *What:* a foreground mass passes the lens and hides the change behind it.
- *Why now:* the object wipe is a staple of short-form transitions (practitioner catalogue,
  `../research/animatic_mograph.md` §3, reported there); Arcane's eclipse wipes (T5); the S5 chimney pot.
- *Ours:* the D carrier: a soft, flat near mass (tier C, our own organic shapes) covering >= 70 % of the stage on the
  cut frame, on screen <= 0.5 s, with its own window of 8-12 frames at up to ~90 px per frame (`../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md`
  S5); moving the way the camera travels; passing under the captions, which stay in screen space and keep their flat
  field. Extends C2.

**M23 Sound leads the cut.** YES.
- *What:* the next shot's sound (for us the voice's next sentence) starts at or just before the picture changes.
- *Why now:* when the audio was removed from match-action cuts, viewers noticed the cuts far more: sound played a large
  part in hiding them (STUDY [N6]); sound is one of the cues that carry attention over a cut (STUDY and theory [N5]).
- *Ours:* DIRECTION 1 already puts a join's midpoint on the next sentence's first word, and the picture leads its word
  by ~0.1 s (DIRECTION 8); a join never falls in a pause of the voice longer than ~0.3 s (est.); a soft sound may mark a
  portal or a wipe (the playbook's "with its own sound" for opening effects). Extends every join.

**M24 The morph: one thing becomes the next, in stages.** ADAPT (sparingly: costly).
- *What:* an object transforms into the next scene's object (a window becomes a bar of a chart, a drop becomes the sea).
- *Why now:* animated transitions improved viewers' tracking of objects and their judgment of change over static jumps,
  and staging the change (one property at a time) helped the tracking (STUDY [N23]); animation helps only when it
  matches the concept and is slow and simple enough to be seen (STUDY review [N22]); shape morphs are the most
  complicated join in the motion-design catalogue (`../research/animatic_mograph.md` §3).
- *Ours:* a B or E carrier when the story says "this IS that": one object, staged: first its position (it moves to where
  the next object will be), then its shape and colour, then the next world arrives round it; 0.4-0.8 s in all (est.);
  the object stays the brightest, hottest thing through the morph; never two morphs in a row. Extends D6.

### E. Explainer composition: what the learning evidence adds

**M25 Coherence: no seductive detail.** YES.
- *What:* everything in the frame serves the beat; interesting but irrelevant extras are cut.
- *Why now:* a meta-analysis of 39 effects found that seductive details lower recall and transfer, more when they are
  pictures and when study time is limited (a video sets its own pace); a 2020 meta-analysis confirmed the harm (META,
  from summaries [N18]).
- *Ours:* every named thing in the frame is the beat's subject, the thread object, one big quiet secondary mass or 1-3
  supporting things (<= 6 named objects: DIRECTION 2); everything else is texture inside a mass, at low contrast; Pip is
  the one sanctioned find and stays off the eye path (>= 250 px from the focal); no joke props, no logos, no readable
  fine print. Extends the playbook's SCENE COMPOSITION.

**M26 Signalling: one highlight, on its word.** YES.
- *What:* the essential is cued (by light, colour, motion, a highlight, the voice's stress) at the moment it is said.
- *Why now:* signalling raised retention (g = 0.53) and transfer (g = 0.33) across 145 comparisons, and later
  meta-analyses agree (META, from summaries [N19]); the picture with its word (temporal contiguity) is one of the
  mechanisms the drawing study names (STUDY [N20]).
- *Ours:* one signal at a time, and all of a beat's signals on the same word: the caption's keyword highlight, the story
  light's peak, the accent's arrival, the push; the picture ~0.1 s ahead of the word (DIRECTION 8); no arrows or
  callout boxes (our signals are light, colour and motion). Extends E2, E8.

**M27 The live drawing, with a visible agent.** YES.
- *What:* the picture is drawn while you watch, with the drawer's hand or tool in view.
- *Why now:* watching an instructor's hand draw a diagram raised transfer over the same diagram shown already drawn (d =
  0.35); the drawing appearing with no hand in view did not (d = -0.16); the hand alone beat the instructor's whole body
  (d = 0.36); the hand-versus-no-hand contrast was never tested inside one experiment (STUDY, from summaries [N20]).
  Whiteboard animations raised engagement, enjoyment and retention, and short course videos with hand-drawn tablet
  sketches were among the most engaging (STUDY, from summaries [N21]). The owner's reels teach "the line first, then
  the world under it" (`reels.md` §5).
- *Ours:* frame 0's draw-on keeps the playbook's moving pen tip, at full contrast, the line the darkest mark in the
  frame, lasting >= 1 s and peaking after 0.4 s (`vertical.md` V9); after frame 0 a draw-on only where the drawing is
  the beat (DIRECTION 8), with its pen tip in view. The tip is our visible agent; whether a tip alone carries the hand's
  effect is untested (est.). Extends the reels' own device.

**M28 Congruence and apprehension: animate the real mechanism, slowly enough to see.** YES.
- *What:* the motion shows the structure of what it explains (cause, then effect, one tick per unit) at a speed the eye
  can follow.
- *Why now:* animation beats still pictures only when it matches the structure of the concept (congruence) and can be
  perceived accurately (apprehension); many animations are too fast or too complex (STUDY review [N22]); staged
  transitions improved tracking (STUDY [N23]).
- *Ours:* `../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md`'s "Show the real mechanism": each unit of cause is one visible tick of the effect;
  each step of a mechanism holds ~0.4 s or more unless it is one of many that read together as a flow (est.); one causal
  chain at a time; cause and effect in one frame where they can be (SR11, SR13). New to the reels.

**M29 Words few and big, in one home.** YES (already ours).
- *What:* on-screen words as a designed element: few, large, in one fixed place, in step with the voice.
- *Why now:* text pulls the eye almost as hard as a face (11.1x a control region) and is hard to ignore (STUDY [N14]);
  text draws attention in proportion to its size (STUDY [N17]); kinetic typography heads every 2025-2026 trend roundup
  (ONE SOURCE each, blogs [N41]).
- *Ours:* captions only in the band (98 px, <= 2 lines, lines <= 780 px: `vertical.md` V5), popping in place on the
  voice; in the world at most one label or one number (>= 56 px; a number 120-160 px), on the subject; no title over the
  picture (the playbook); no kinetic type flying through the stage: it would be a second subject. Extends the playbook's
  "words in the picture are few and big".

### 6.3 How animation fits inside a composition

1. **One capture lever at a time, at the subject** (§1): its onset, its push, its light and its accent arrive together
   on its word; nothing else starts then.
2. **Either the camera holds and the subject travels the device's line** (E9), **or the camera moves and the subject is
   the still pivot** (E3); never both moving freely.
3. **Ration the onsets:** one noticeable onset at a time; supporting life slow, low in contrast, taking turns; nothing
   starts in the 0.4 s round a join (DIRECTION 3).
4. **Ease like current motion design** (M15): in decelerating, out accelerating, on arcs; position and scale only, never
   opacity.
5. **Drawn things step, the camera glides** (M16): drawn poses held ~5 frames at 60 fps, the camera, the parallax, the
   light and the captions on every frame.
6. **Draw on only where the drawing is the beat** (frame 0, a chart line in step with the voice), with the pen tip in
   view (M27).
7. **Novelty from the subject's own build** every ~1-2 s, never from extra life (the playbook; M12).
8. **Every move is three designed frames** (start, middle, end), each passing the composition rules as a still; the
   caption band a fixed mat throughout (C12; CINEMATIC_GUIDE rule 2).

## 8. Doc clashes and gaps (flagged for the orchestrator; not edited here)

1. **The eye-trace distance across a join.** Only `../research/cine_light.md` T1 gives a number (a light match keeps the
   bright shape within 0.1 H = 190 px); `../CRAFT.md` §a's carriers and G3_ANIMATIC §4 give none for the other joins.
   **Proposal (M19):** the next focal within 150 px of the last (preferred), 190 px for a light match, 370 px at most
   for any carried join; beyond that only on an F cut. Target: `../CRAFT.md` §a, G3_ANIMATIC §4.
2. **The pen tip.** The playbook (Hook and pacing) asks for "a moving pen tip"; DIRECTION rule 9 and
   `../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` "The opening" describe the line drawing on without naming the tip. Fiorella and Mayer's
   result (the drawing helped with the drawer's hand in view, not without it) is a reason to name the visible agent in
   DIRECTION 9. A gap, not a clash.
3. **"A new element every 3-6 frames"** (`../CINEMATIC_GUIDE.md` "The opening", measured on the owner's reference)
   gives no frame rate: at 30 fps it is every 0.1-0.2 s, at our 60 fps it would be every 0.05-0.1 s. **Proposal:**
   state it in seconds (every ~0.1-0.2 s), inside ONE growing group (the subject's build), never as separate calls
   (M28's apprehension).
4. **Easing and fades.** Interface motion systems fade things in and out; DIRECTION 8 forbids opacity fades. M15 takes

5. **Short-siding against lead room.** `cinema.md` C9 and `../COMPOSITION_SHOTS.md` rule 7 ask 1.5-2x more room in front

6. **A sideways speed budget.** No doc gives sideways motion its own budget; M18's "about half the px per frame" is an

7. **The F cut's size change** (`../COMPOSITION_SHOTS.md` §5 row 14: G3_ANIMATIC >= 20 % against `cinema.md` C4 >=

## 9. Our arithmetic (est.)

- **Angles on the owner's feed** (`vertical.md` §3.2: 74 px = 1 degree at 32 cm): 150 px = 2.0 degrees (about the eye's
  sharp centre), 190 px = 2.6, 370 px = 5.0 (the preview field round a fixation). A saccade to a new spot takes ~0.2 s
  to start (a general figure, from the summary of [N9]).
- **The crop-safe stage** (M1): x 130-870 by y 600-1519, from the feed's visible x 98-982, Instagram's rail (x > 870
  below y 860), TikTok's (x > 945 from y 790), the app's text (from y 1519) and the cover's 4:5 crop (y 285-1635), which
  contains it; above y 860 the right edge may run to x 945 (`vertical.md` V8).
- **Area shares** (the frame is 2,073,600 px²): a 180 px standing figure (~60 px wide) ~10,800 px² = 0.5 %; a 150 x 230
  px lit window 34,500 px² = 1.7 %; an accent of 3-5 % = 62,000-104,000 px² (a patch of ~250 x 250 to ~320 x 320 px).
- **Stepped animation at 60 fps:** twos at 24 fps = 2/24 s = 83 ms = 5 frames; threes = 125 ms = 7.5 frames.
- **Easing durations at 60 fps:** 250 ms = 15 frames, 400 ms = 24, 450 ms = 27; 150-250 ms = 9-15.
- **Sideways against vertical:** the visible width over the height, 884 / 1920 = 0.46: a mover at the same px per frame
  crosses the width 2.2x sooner.
- **The hole rule** (M17): for a camera move T (in world units), a plane at depth z shifts f T / z px on screen (f the
  focal length in px); between planes at z1 < z2 a strip f T (1/z1 - 1/z2) wide is uncovered. Example with
  `../LAYOUT.md` §8.2's float factors (near 1, plane 0 0.6, plane +1 0.24): a 60 px drift of the near plane moves plane
  0 by 36 px, uncovering 24 px of it, so plane 0 is painted >= 24 + 40 = 64 px past the near plane's edge; plane +1
  moves 14 px, so it is painted >= 22 + 40 = 62 px past plane 0's edge. (`lib/depth.ts`'s own constants differ from
  that table: a flagged tooling item there.)
- **Shot lengths for scale:** 13 s (1945) -> 6.8 s (1965) -> ~4 s (1985, 2005) in the sampled films [N2]; our panels
  average >= 3.5 s (DIRECTION 1), close to the modern feature's shot, while the changes inside them come every ~1-2 s.
