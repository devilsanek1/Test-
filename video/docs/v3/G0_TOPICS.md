## A. Meta: 43 million violations in a state of 2 million

**Big question.** Can anyone actually make Big Tech pay for what it does with our data?

**So-what chain.** A judge ordered Meta to pay $25B, so what? The number comes from counting each misleading
statement (about privacy and content moderation) once for every New Mexican in its audience, about 43-44 million violations at up to $5,000 each. So what?
That's an old consumer-fraud law, not a new privacy law, and other states have similar laws they can use the same way. So what?
A privacy promise in an app now has a possible price per person who saw it. That gives companies a reason to word
the pop-ups and settings pages you read much more carefully.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Meta got another huge fine. They always do, nothing changes."
- During: "Wait, 43 million violations in a state of 2 million? How?" Then: "Oh, it's people times promises."
- After: "The size isn't the story, the maths is. Every user who was misled counts, and any state can copy that."
- Tell-a-friend version: "A jury counted every New Mexican in the audience of Facebook's misleading promises as a separate violation,
  which is how one small state got to $25 billion."

**Beat outline (about 40 s, roughly 110 spoken words).**
| Time | Beat | Line idea | Key image |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | Hook | "About 2 million people live in New Mexico. A jury just found Meta broke the law there more than 43 million times." | Frame 0: the text on screen, and a desert welcome sign "POP. 2,117,522" with a tally counter already spinning past it. |
| 3-8 s | Stakes | "The bill: $25 billion. Meta says it'll appeal." | **Scale moment:** the counter spins up, a tower of coins rises over the whole small state on a map at dusk. |
| 8-22 s | Explanation | "A jury looked at 29 things Meta said publicly about your data and what's allowed on Facebook, and found 26 misleading. Then it counted each one once for every New Mexican in its audience." | **Cozy enclosure:** one person at a kitchen table under a lamp, phone showing a privacy pop-up. The camera pulls out, the room repeats into a grid of identical kitchens, and each one gets a tick. |
| 22-32 s | Twist | "People times promises. That's how you get 20 times the population. And the max was about $220 billion; the judge picked $25." | A multiplication: one person x 26 speech bubbles = a wall of ticks. Then a giant $220B bar beside a smaller $25B bar. |
| 32-40 s | Payoff | "The law it used isn't a new privacy law. It's an old consumer-fraud law other states have too. So the next promise an app makes you could come with a price per person." | Back to the kitchen pop-up, now with a tiny "$5,000" tag on a string. Hold, end. |

**Facts, sources, dates.**
- 21 Oct 2026: Judge Francis Mathew ordered Meta to pay $25B under New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act, with 15% annual
  interest until paid; under the state constitution consumer-protection penalties go to education (AG: schools). Sources: CNN 2026-10-21 (cnn.com/2026/10/21/tech/meta-25-billion-new-mexico-judgment),
  NBC News, Washington Post 2026-10-21, Source NM 2026-10-21 (sourcenm.com/2026/10/21/new-mexico-judge-orders-meta-to-pay-state-25b/).
- 25 Sep 2026: a Santa Fe jury found 26 of 29 statements misleading (on data shared with third parties, i.e. Cambridge Analytica, and on removing hate speech and misinformation) and set the count, using each statement's audience (NM population ~2.1M or ~1.36M Facebook users). Sources: Santa Fe New Mexican, Source NM 2026-09-25.
- About 43-44M violations at up to $5,000 each, a maximum of about $219-220B. The state asked for $35-40B and Meta proposed $3.45B.
  Sources: Source NM, NM DOJ press release (nmdoj.gov).
- Meta says it will appeal ("confident we will prevail"). No appeal bond reported for this case: the $1.8B bond belongs to the separate March child-safety case. AG Torrez: "one of the single largest, if not the largest, single-state verdict against a company in American history."
- New Mexico population about 2.1M (2020 census: 2,117,522), our own added context. Judge 2 confirmed the core facts in a search across AP,
  Source NM and Bloomberg Tax.

**Uncertain or disputed.**
- 43M is the state's estimate; the judge's order says "nearly 44M". Script says "more than 43 million".
- The deceptive statements covered hate speech and content as well as data (judge 2). Check the order's exact wording before
  saying "privacy promises" alone. CNN's headline links it to Cambridge Analytica; confirm that in the order.
- "Other states can copy it" is our reading of the mechanism. Phrase it as "other states have similar laws", not "will copy".
- Don't confuse it with the separate March 2026 NM verdict ($375M penalties + $567M abatement, child safety; that's where the $1.8B bond is).
- Meta's strongest point: the jury made no finding that any New Mexican actually saw or was harmed by the statements; Reuters-cited experts expect the $25B to be cut on appeal. Never say "43 million people were misled".

**Fairness notes.** Always "ordered to pay, under appeal", never "paid". Include Meta's position in one line (it says the
AG mixes up privacy and speech, calls it a tax on speech, and expects to win on appeal). No villain; the story is the
counting method.

**Fact status:** 21 Oct judgment, $25B, $5,000/violation, ~$219B max, $35-40B asked vs ~$3.4B offered, 15% interest, 26 of 29 statements, 25 Sep jury, ~43-44M violations re-confirmed 2026-10-25 (NBC, ABC, PBS, ABQ Journal, Bloomberg Law, Source NM 25 Sep). Fixed: jury (not the state) set the count; statements cover privacy and hate speech/misinformation, so "privacy promises" alone is now "promises"; "who heard it" is now "in its audience" (no finding anyone saw them); $1.8B bond belongs to the March case. Open: whether the 43M figure is population- or Facebook-user-based per statement (both reported); Cambridge Analytica link is in AP/ABC coverage but not checked in the order itself.

## B. AI is outbidding you for the memory in your laptop

**Big question.** Is AI going to make my everyday life more expensive?

**So-what chain.** PC shipments fell about 21% in a quarter, so what? Makers raised prices because memory chips cost about 5x more.
So what? Memory makers are moving factory lines to the high-end memory AI data centres buy, because those buyers pay more.
So what? Less ordinary memory is left for laptops and phones, and memory and storage are now about 40% of what a PC costs to build
(about 15% normally). So what? Computers, which almost always got cheaper, cost about 10% more than a year ago (US CPI). Someone
replacing a dead laptop this Christmas pays more or gets less, and analysts don't expect relief before 2027-28.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Laptops seem pricier. Inflation, I guess."
- During: "Wait, computers went *up*? And it's because of AI?"
- After: "There's an auction for memory chips, and data centres outbid me without me knowing I was bidding."
- Tell-a-friend version: "AI data centres are buying up the memory chips, so laptops got pricier this year for the first time in ages."

**Beat outline (about 40 s).**
| Time | Beat | Line idea | Key image |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | Hook | "For 20 years computers kept getting cheaper. In the past year they went up almost 10%." | Frame 0: the text, and a price line on a chalkboard sliding downhill for 20 years (one small bump around 2021), then snapping upward. |
| 3-8 s | Stakes | "If your laptop dies before Christmas, you'll pay more, or get less." | **Cozy enclosure:** a kid at a desk under a lamp, an old laptop with a cracked hinge, a price tag on a new one that keeps growing. |
| 8-22 s | Explanation | "Inside every laptop is memory, RAM. AI data centres need mountains of it. The few companies that make it are selling to whoever pays most, and that's not you." | **Scale moment:** a data centre drawn as a whale swallowing a glowing river of tiny RAM sticks, a small laptop on the bank getting a trickle. |
| 22-32 s | Twist | "So it's less a shortage than an auction, and you were in it without knowing. Memory now makes up about 40% of what it costs to build a PC." | An auction hall: a giant hand with a paddle (the data centre) against a tiny hand (you). A PC cut open, the memory slice growing to 40%. |
| 32-40 s | Payoff | "The world bought about a fifth fewer computers last quarter. Not mainly because people stopped wanting them: prices went up, and the cheap ones are going first." | Shop shelf, the cheap laptops' price cards flipping to "sold out". End. |

**Facts, sources, dates.**
- 12 Oct 2026: Gartner says Q3 worldwide PC shipments were -22.4% (55.4M); analyst Namrata Banerjee says DRAM costs roughly
  quintupled year on year in Q2 and were passed on to buyers. HP -30.9%. Source: Gartner newsroom 2026-10-12. Judge 2 confirmed it.
- Same week: IDC -20.1% (62.7M), Omdia -21.2% (58.1M, forecasts -24% Q4), Counterpoint -21% (57M, reported 23 Oct, forecasts -27% Q4).
  Sources: Tom's Hardware; Technology.org 2026-10-23; 24/7 Wall St. 2026-10-24.
- Omdia: memory and storage are nearly 40% of PC build cost vs about 15% normally.
- 14 Oct 2026 CPI (September): "computers, peripherals and smart home assistants" +9.7% y/y, said to be the largest since at least 2006.
  +9.7% (relative importance 0.319) CONFIRMED in the BLS Sept 2026 CPI release table (bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm). "Largest since at least 2006" is still secondary (NBC News; ROIC.ai 2026-10-14).
- Intel raised CPU prices about 10% on 5 Oct, its third hike of 2026 (supporting fact only).
- Next fresh peg: SK hynix Q3 results 27-28 Oct.

**Uncertain or disputed.**
- +9.7% holds (BLS). "Largest since 2006" still needs the BLS series (CUUR0000SEEE01) before it goes on screen; until then don't say it.
- "Cheaper every year for 20 years" is FALSE as stated: Counterpoint (Jun 2026) says the index rose year on year once before in 15 years (pandemic, ~2021). Use "kept getting cheaper" / "almost every year"; check the BLS series for the exact bump.
- IDC partly blames stockpiling in the first half of the year (demand pulled forward), so don't claim the drop is purely price. All CPI figures are US only; say so on screen if the audience is global. The Fed reportedly flags quality-adjustment quirks in computer prices.
- "Cheap models disappear first" is reported but is a trend, not a measured number; keep it soft ("the cheap ones go first").

**Fairness notes.** No villains: memory makers selling to the highest bidder is normal. Never "AI is raising prices on everything"
(computers are about 0.3% of the CPI). It's AI-adjacent after video 2, but it's a prices story, not an AI-ethics one.

**Fact status:** Gartner -22.4% / 55.4M / DRAM ~5x (Q2) / HP -30.9%, IDC -20.1% / 62.7M (partly stockpiling), Omdia -21.2% / 58.1M / -24% Q4 / ~40% BOM, and BLS Sept CPI computers +9.7% re-confirmed 2026-10-25. Fixed: "cheaper every year for 20 years" (false: the index also rose around 2021) to "kept getting cheaper"; "not a shortage" to "less a shortage than an auction" (every source calls it a shortage); payoff no longer claims demand played no role. Open: "largest since 2006", Counterpoint 57M/-27% Q4, Omdia's "~15% normally", Intel 5 Oct +10%, "cheap models go first" (anecdotal).

## C. The tumour that can't spell

**Big question.** More young people are getting bowel and rectal cancer. Is treatment getting any less brutal?

**So-what chain.** The FDA approved a drug on 19 Oct, so what? For one type of locally advanced rectal cancer it can be used
alone, without chemo, radiation or surgery up front. So what? Standard treatment can mean a permanent colostomy bag,
infertility and sexual problems. So what? In the trial, the tumour disappeared and stayed gone for a year in about half
(47% by independent review, 68% by the trial doctors). So what? For the right patient, a single test on the biopsy can decide
between major surgery and a course of infusions.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Cancer treatment means surgery and chemo. That's just how it is."
- During: "Wait, the drug doesn't even attack the cancer?"
- After: "Some tumours are full of DNA typos, which makes them easy for the immune system to spot. The drug just takes the
  brake off. That's why the test matters."
- Tell-a-friend version: "For one kind of rectal cancer, a drug that releases the immune system's brake made the tumour vanish
  in about half the patients, no surgery, but only if a test shows that type."

**Beat outline (about 42 s).**
| Time | Beat | Line idea | Key image |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | Hook | "For one type of rectal cancer, one drug made the tumour disappear in about half the patients. No surgery." | Frame 0: the text, and a tumour on a scan fading out like ink in water. |
| 3-9 s | Stakes | "Normally that's chemo, radiation and surgery, and for some people a bag for life." | A hospital timeline with three heavy doors closing one after another. |
| 9-24 s | Explanation | "These tumours have a broken spell-checker for their DNA. So they're full of typos, and to your immune system, typos look foreign. It holds back because the tumour presses its brake." | **Cozy enclosure:** inside a cell, a tiny scribe copying DNA by lamplight, pages covered in red typos; outside the window immune guards stand next to a brake lever with a tumour's foot on it. |
| 24-33 s | Twist | "The drug doesn't attack the cancer at all. It just takes the foot off the brake." | The foot lifts. **Scale moment:** one guard becomes an army pouring across the tissue landscape. |
| 33-42 s | Payoff | "It's a small group, roughly 5 to 10 in 100 rectal cancers. But one test on the biopsy tells you if you're in it, and that test can decide whether you need the surgery." | Back to the small lab: one slide under a microscope, a label "dMMR? yes". End. |

**Facts, sources, dates.**
- 19 Oct 2026: FDA accelerated approval for GSK's dostarlimab (Jemperli) alone in previously untreated stage II-III dMMR/MSI-H locally
  advanced rectal cancer. Healio calls it the first immunotherapy approved for this. Sources: Targeted Oncology 2026-10-19,
  CancerNetwork 2026-10-19, OncLive 2026-10-21, TIME 2026-10-21, Medscape.
- AZUR-1 phase 2 (154 patients): clinical complete response held 12 months in 47% (54/116) by independent central review and
  68% (98/144) by investigators; response rate 79%. Of 15 who had surgery anyway, 12 had no cancer left; 71% of the 154 got the drug alone with no other treatment. Re-confirmed
  2026-10-25 via ASCO Post, Targeted Oncology, CancerNetwork.
- Background: MSK trial (NCT04165772): 2022, all of the first 12 patients cleared (NEJM); 2024 update, all 42 who finished treatment
  had no evidence of tumour (GSK/ASCO 2024). FDA also reviewed it: 93% cCR12 in 50 patients.
- The mechanism (mismatch repair deficiency, PD-1 brake) is textbook; verify the wording against MSK or NCI patient pages.

**Uncertain or disputed.**
- Accelerated approval on a single-arm phase 2 with short follow-up; long-term relapse risk unknown, confirmatory trial required.
- Subtype size: 5-10% of rectal cancers is the sourced figure (approval coverage). The "FDA about 3%" figure could not be found; drop it
  unless someone locates it.
- The cause of rising young-onset cancer is unknown; pose the question, don't answer it.

**Fairness notes.** Never "cure", never "cancer vanished" without "one type" and "about half". Never the MSK "100%" (12 in 2022, 42 in 2024) without the
2026 47-68%. No "Big Pharma" framing, no promises to viewers. Add a quiet on-screen line: "Talk to your doctor; this applies to
one tested subtype." Freshness: the approval is 6 days old, inside the 10-day exceptional window.

**Fact status:** approval (19 Oct 2026), AZUR-1 numbers (47% IRC, 79% ORR, 12/15 pCR, 71% drug alone) and MSK 2024 (42/42) re-confirmed 2026-10-25. Fixed: "bag for life" now "for some people"; subtype 5-10% of rectal cancers; "only reason" overclaim softened. Open: the 68% investigator figure (98/144) is from scouting only; the 95% CI 27-56% reported for 47% looks odd, check the label before showing any CI; mechanism wording vs NCI/MSK still to check.
