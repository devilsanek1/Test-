## Pick one

**1. The AI that called the hurricane** (70/90)
- What: Hurricane Isaias hit near Destin, Florida, on 9 Oct (Category 2). NPR, 26 Oct: the usual models first said Louisiana; Google DeepMind's AI said Florida's Panhandle from the start.
- What AI did: it got the path right, not the strength. People still issue the warnings; the US hurricane center blends this AI into its official forecast.
- Core line: "The usual forecast models said Louisiana, and Google's AI said Florida." Cover: MODELS SAID LOUISIANA. AI SAID FLORIDA. Line 2: "And it was right. It pointed there before the storm even had a name."
- Hopeful: it's helping now, in forecasts people already watch, and it's improving fast. Hurricane expert Michael Lowry: "the best performing computer model that we've ever seen. Period."
- Risk: one storm proves little alone. Nine people died, so the tone stays gentle. Not a medicine story.
- Fact status: CHECKED (NPR 26 Oct, Wikipedia, CNN 8 Oct). Fixed: "every forecast said Louisiana" was false. Open: how many days of warning it gained.

**2. A virus designed by AI, sent after a superbug** (68/90)
- What: on 20 Oct, start-up Radical Numerics said doctors gave a patient its AI-designed phages. The patient's E. coli had beaten antibiotics and every available phage (NYT).
- What AI did: it redrew the legs of a known bacteria-eating virus (T7) to grab this patient's strain. In a lab dish, the mix wiped it out. The patient's outcome is private.
- Core line: "Antibiotics couldn't stop this superbug, so doctors tried an AI-designed virus." Cover: DOCTORS TRIED AN AI-DESIGNED VIRUS. Line 2: "It's a virus that only eats bacteria, and an AI redrew its legs to grab this one."
- Hopeful: superbugs kill over a million people a year. Matching a phage by hand is slow; its maker thinks AI can do it "in days".
- Risk: it's the company's word. No peer review, no hospital named, no outcome yet. Harvard's Michael Baym: "If it works, that's incredible."
- Fact status: CHECKED (company report, NYT, The Register). Fixed: E. coli, T7 and the FDA's OK come from the company, via the NYT. No update as of today.

**3. Cancer hiding in "normal" scans** (65/90)
- What: Mayo Clinic's AI, REDMOD, in the journal Gut. Back in the news on 26 Oct (a BMJ press release); the paper is from April.
- What AI did: on old CT scans of 219 people later diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, it flagged 73%, about 16 months early on average. Radiologists caught 39%.
- Core line: "These scans looked normal. An AI found cancer in most of them." Cover: AI FOUND CANCER IN "NORMAL" SCANS. Line 2: "It was pancreatic cancer, on average about 16 months before anyone diagnosed it."
- Hopeful: this cancer is usually found too late. The warning could come from a scan people already had.
- Risk: April science, not this week's. Old scans only; about 1 in 5 healthy scans flagged by mistake; not in clinics yet.
- Fact status: CHECKED (BMJ via ScienceDaily, 26 Oct). Fixed: radiologists did see some (39%), so never "invisible to humans". Open: no date for the real-patient study.

**Recommendation: 1, the hurricane.** The only one already helping people, on solid sources, with the clearest picture. For a medicine story, pick 2, and we say plainly that we don't know yet if it worked.

## Fact-check notes (sources read 2026-10-28; feeds the fact lock)

**1. Hurricane.** NPR (Jessica Meszaros, 26 Oct, read via text.npr.org/nx-s1-6012820): "Conventional forecasting models
initially predicted it would head further west, toward southeastern Louisiana"; Google DeepMind "called it right out of
the gate: the hurricane was going to hit the western Florida Panhandle"; NHC "first started incorporating the Google
DeepMind model into its official forecasts last year"; it "didn't do as well as the NHC's blended forecast in
determining the intensity". Lowry, verbatim: "That model has been, by far and away, the best performing computer model
that we've ever seen." / "Period." / "The AI models don't really know anything about the atmosphere other than the
current picture" / a year's gains "that would have taken us 10 years, a decade or more, to make". McNoldy: "It makes us
a little uncomfortable, because we like to know why you're getting the right answer". Wikipedia (citing CNN, Andrew
Freedman, 8 Oct): GFS and IFS pointed to "a landfall in Louisiana or Mississippi as a tropical storm"; "The AI models,
such as Google DeepMind's model, preferred a landfall location in Alabama or Florida", before the storm formed (Tropical
Depression Nine: 6 Oct, 21:00 UTC; named 7 Oct); landfall near Destin at 01:30 UTC 10 Oct (evening of 9 Oct, local),
Category 2, 105 mph; nine deaths (six in Florida, two in Alabama, one in South Carolina); insured losses over $2 bn;
hurricane watches for Alabama, Mississippi and the Florida Panhandle on 7 Oct. Fixed: the scout's "Every forecast said
Louisiana" (other AI models agreed with Florida; the physics models said "Louisiana or Mississippi"). Before formation the
AI favoured "Alabama or Florida", so line 2 says "pointed there", never "named Destin". Name Google once, plainly (v2 was
the Google-staff story). Open: the lead time in days; NHC's own first track; the season's accuracy figures (James
Franklin via Michael Lowry's Substack, data to 6 Sept) are informal.

**2. Phage.** Company report (Radical Numerics, 20 Oct; a "Research Preview" blog, not peer reviewed): "In the world's
first clinical use of AI-generated phages, Omnii-1 designs were administered to a human patient under compassionate
use"; "an authorized, physician-led compassionate-use pathway"; "the cocktail containing Omnii-1-designed phage variants
eliminated the patient's antibiotic-resistant isolate in vitro"; "we cannot disclose the patient's clinical course or
outcomes". It names neither the bacterium, nor the base phage (T7 is its worked example), nor the FDA. NYT (via the
Boston Globe, 20 Oct; headline "Startup claims to use AI-created viruses to treat a patient"): "a patient with a stubborn
E. coli infection"; "The bacteria had resisted not just antibiotics but all the available phages"; "Company officials
said the trial had been authorized by the Food and Drug Administration"; "variations of a phage called T7", "altering
the shapes of T7's legs"; "The report has not been peer reviewed". Eric Nguyen (CEO): "We think we can get it done in
days." Dr. Marisa Azad (Ottawa Hospital, working with the company): "Hunting for phages is remarkably time-consuming."
Michael Baym (Harvard, not involved): "If it works, that's incredible." / "My impulse is to be skeptical." The Register
(22 Oct): no outcome; the company "didn't respond to questions"; "observations from a single patient don't establish
much of anything". A search today found no outcome or update. "Over a million deaths a year": Semafor 28 Oct (per the
scout) and the GRAM study (Lancet, 2024: 1.14 million deaths attributable to resistant infections in 2021); not
re-checked today. Fixed: "every antibiotic" became "antibiotics" (the NYT's word); "first", E. coli, T7 and the FDA's
OK stay attributed to the company.

**3. Pancreatic.** BMJ Group release via ScienceDaily (26 Oct), Gut (gutjnl-2025-337266): 219 patients; scans an average
of 475 days before diagnosis; "a sensitivity of 73%, compared with 39% for radiologists"; for scans more than 2 years
out, "68%, nearly three times the 23% recorded by radiologists"; "correctly classified just over 81% of scans as cancer
free" (539 people) and 87.5% on the NIH set (80); repeat scans matched "in 90 to 92% of cases"; limits: not ethnically
diverse, "must also undergo prospective testing", "more research is necessary before it can be introduced into routine
medical care". No named person. Fixed: the scout's twist "nobody missed anything a human could see" is too strong
(radiologists caught 39%): say the AI caught almost twice as many. Open: the April online date (Europe PMC, per the
scout); no date for Mayo's prospective study.

**Runner-up check, Microsoft's DxO.** TechCrunch (22 Oct) and Medical Xpress (23 Oct): 83.2% vs 36.1% for "primary care
doctors and general internists, working individually with reference materials", in a simulation; specialists and teams
"weren't part of the comparison"; GPT-5 alone scored 78.9% at about $8,400 of tests per case, and DxO cut "about $3,000"
and added "about four points"; "much of the improvement comes from more powerful underlying AI models". So the scout's
twist (it won by acting like a careful team) does not hold.
