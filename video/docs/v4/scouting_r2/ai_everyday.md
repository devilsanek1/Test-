## 1. Your AI friend's company went bankrupt. Who gets your chats?

- **News:** Character Technologies (Character.AI) filed for Chapter 11 on **12 Oct 2026** (Delaware, case 26-11603). Disney is the stalking-horse bidder: **$20 M** for a non-exclusive licence to some of the company's IP. The rest of the assets go to auction. About **40 state attorneys general** have asked the court to set conditions on any sale: users get to delete their data before it is transferred, and chats can't be used for training without consent. The proposed timeline: sale-rules hearing **30 Oct**, bids **~20 Nov**, auction **~23 Nov**. If no buyer keeps the platform running, it shuts down.
- **Big question:** What happens to the most private thing you've ever typed when the app you typed it into goes bust?
- **Twist:** Nobody has bid for the chats yet. The company's lawyer told the court that no buyer has offered for the user data or for the chatbot business. Disney's licence reportedly excludes user chats. Still, the privacy policy allows user data to be disclosed in a sale. So the chats are not "for sale to Disney" (that would be an exaggeration). Their fate is simply undecided, and that decision is made in a bankruptcy court, not by the users.
- **So what:** Millions of people, at least ~2 M of them kids per KQED, confided in these bots → the company's assets now get sold to pay its debts → the privacy policy already allows data to move with a sale → your chats could pass to a new owner, or the companion you talk to every night could just switch off → the one thing you control is deleting or exporting before the sale.
- **Thesis:** Everyone is talking about AI companions being risky for teens. This bankruptcy shows that what you tell an AI friend counts as a company asset, and that matters because in a sale, assets follow the privacy policy, not your feelings.
- **Frame 0:** "Your AI friend's company just went bankrupt"
- **Hooks:** "You told it your secrets. Now it's in a bankruptcy court." / "What happens to your chats when the AI company goes bust?" / "Millions of people talked to this app every night. It might switch off by Christmas."
- **Real person:** No named user found yet. Possible: an AG statement (to find), or Character.AI's statement that user data "remains protected under its privacy policy" (per CBS).
- **Share reason:** "Delete your c.ai account before the auction" is practical advice a friend or parent will pass on.
- **Key image:** A cozy, dense bedroom at night: a kid whispering to a glowing phone character. Pull back: the phone is a tiny box on a conveyor in a huge auction warehouse full of crates labelled "chats".
- **Evidence:** Solid on the filing, the Disney bid and the AG filing (TechCrunch, CBS, Variety, KQED). The user count is disputed: KQED says ~12 M monthly, Tech Times 21.6 M. The schedule dates come from secondary blogs, so check the Stretto docket.
- **Risks:** About 15 days old. The 30 Oct hearing is the fresh peg, so recheck it then. Do not say "Disney is buying your chats". Teen lawsuits sit in the background, so keep it from turning into a villain story.

## 2. One in three young adults asks a chatbot about drugs, and nobody can see what it said

- **News:** In a Rutgers Health study (JMIR Formative Research, reported **26 Oct 2026**), nearly 1,000 US adults aged 18-29 were surveyed. **Over 32 %** had used generative AI for drug or alcohol information, about half of them at least monthly, and they reported high trust in the answers. Companion piece: a *Nature Health* perspective (Binghamton, Stanford, Texas A&M, Indiana; **9 Oct 2026**) argues that harm from chatbot health advice is "structurally hidden" from doctors and regulators.
- **Big question:** Is it safe to ask AI about your health?
- **Twist:** The danger isn't only wrong answers. When one goes wrong, the conversation is invisible. In the *Nature Health* paper's case, a 60-year-old man asked ChatGPT how to cut chloride, replaced table salt with sodium bromide, and was hospitalised with bromide poisoning. His doctors **could not see what he had been told**, because he couldn't retrieve the chat. There is no reporting system and no record. "Nobody, literally no one, is looking at this."
- **So what:** A third of young adults ask a bot instead of a doctor or friend → they trust the confident tone → if the advice is wrong, there's no paper trail for the ER doctor → no one counts the harm, so no one fixes it → the practical habit: save or screenshot the chat and show it to the doctor.
- **Thesis:** Everyone is talking about whether AI gives good health advice. This new research shows that bad advice leaves no trace at all, which matters because the doctor treating you can't see what the bot told you.
- **Frame 0:** "1 in 3 young adults asks AI about drugs"
- **Hooks:** "A third of young adults now ask a chatbot about drugs. Here's the scary part: nobody can see the answers." / "He asked ChatGPT how to cut salt. He ended up in hospital, and his doctors couldn't read the chat." / "Your doctor has your records. Your chatbot doesn't share them."
- **Real person:** Kaicheng Yang (Binghamton): "We're getting medical advice from these chatbots, but nobody—literally no one—is looking at this." Also Mireia Triguero Roura (Rutgers): "Using these tools can lead to misinformed or otherwise risky substance-use behavior among young people."
- **Share reason:** It is useful to a parent of a 20-year-old, and the takeaway is clear: screenshot the chat for your doctor.
- **Key image:** A huge, dense city at night with millions of tiny lit windows, a person in each, whispering to a phone. Inside one cozy dorm room, a speech bubble floats up and dissolves before reaching the hospital across the street.
- **Evidence:** The Rutgers numbers are peer-reviewed (cross-sectional survey, self-reported). The bromide case is a published case report (2025), retold in the Oct 2026 paper. *Nature Health* is a perspective piece, not new data. The survey does not show harm, only use and trust.
- **Risks:** Two sources stitched together, so keep the spine on "invisible advice". Rutgers notes racial differences in use; leave them out or handle with care. Don't imply chatbots are mostly wrong. ChatGPT is named in the case: fine as fact, no villain.
- **Sources:** WRNJ / Rutgers 2026-10-26 https://wrnjradio.com/rutgers-study-finds-1-in-3-young-adults-use-ai-chatbots-for-drug-and-alcohol-information/ · JMIR Formative Research https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e100667 · Medical Xpress / Binghamton 2026-10-09 https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-10-chatbot-doctors-struggling-ai-health.html · Binghamton News https://www.binghamton.edu/news/story/6530/ai-health-chatbots

## 3. Same question, opposite answers: two chatbots asked "who should I vote for?"

- **News:** TechCrunch, **24 Oct 2026**: Convovo (led by Cornell professor **Tapan Parikh**) asked developer-API versions of xAI's Grok 4.7 and OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol to pick one candidate in **nearly 500** midterm races. Grok picked the Republican **92 %** of the time; GPT picked the Democrat **97 %** of the time. They agreed **less than 10 %** of the time. Pew (**23 Oct**) found **8 %** of US adults use chatbots for election info. Election day is **3 Nov**.
- **Big question:** Can you trust an AI to be neutral?
- **Twist:** The two leans come from different places. With web search switched off, GPT still leaned Democrat: Parikh says "it's in the weights". Grok mostly refused, which means its lean came from what it *read*: it cited X in 41 % of answers and a Christian conservative voter guide in 39 %. Each Grok call also carried ~1,240 hidden input tokens the researchers couldn't see. So bias in an AI has two sources: what it learned and what it looks up. Also, the consumer apps refuse the question; the API versions answer it.
- **So what:** The same question gets opposite answers depending on which app you open → an AI's "opinion" is either baked in or comes from whatever sources it searches → apps built on these APIs (not ChatGPT itself) may pass that lean along → if you ask an AI anything contested, ask where it got the answer.
- **Thesis:** Everyone is talking about whether AI is biased. This test shows two models leaning opposite ways for different reasons (one from training, one from what it searches), which matters because the answer you get depends on the app, not the facts.
- **Frame 0:** "Two AIs. Same question. Opposite votes."
- **Hooks:** "We asked two AIs who to vote for. They agreed less than one time in ten." / "One AI picked Republicans 92 % of the time. The other picked Democrats 97 %." / "Your chatbot has a lean, and it got it in one of two ways."
- **Real person:** Tapan Parikh (Cornell / Convovo): "For GPT, it has mostly decided already … it's in the weights." OpenAI spokesperson: ChatGPT is "designed to be objective by default."
- **Share reason:** People will send it to friends on both sides. It's timely before the 3 Nov vote, and it is symmetric.
- **Key image:** Two identical tiny voters at two identical cozy booths in a huge, busy polling hall. Each booth has a little robot helper pointing in opposite directions. Behind each robot: one has a giant library baked into its head, the other is reading a stack of newspapers as they fall in.
- **Evidence:** One study from an independent studio, not peer-reviewed, using the API with a forced-choice prompt. Reported by TechCrunch, heise and AI Insider. The follow-up numbers differ between outlets: TechCrunch says 30 races with search off (GPT 24 D / 1 R / 5 declined; Grok 27 declined), heise reports 387/37/43 and 441 declined. Get the primary write-up.
- **Risks:** This is the most politically sensitive candidate. It must stay strictly symmetric with no "which side is right". Consumer apps refuse, so don't imply ChatGPT tells users how to vote. Partisan comments could swamp the explainer, and the owner prefers wallet stories. After 3 Nov it goes stale.

## 4. (Backup, older) The university gave students an AI tutor. Their grades went down.

- **News:** In a University of Maryland randomised trial (fall 2025; EdWorkingPaper 26-1598, covered **2 Oct 2026**), **2,379** undergraduates in 30 instructors' courses were studied. Students offered a GPT-4o "Virtual Study Assistant" finished **~4 percentage points lower** (0.37 SD). Course-platform participation fell **0.90 SD**. Only **~15 %** used it; **74 %** of requests asked for answers or explanations. First-generation students lost more.
- **Twist:** Most students never touched it, yet grades still fell. The tool crowded out the readings and practice they would otherwise have done.
- **Real person:** Jing Liu (UMD): "when evaluating an AI tool's impact on learning, we need to consider what it might replace."
- **Risks:** **25 days old, so it fails freshness.** Only use it if a new peg appears. It is a working paper, not peer-reviewed. It is a university study, not schools.
- **Sources:** EdTech Innovation Hub https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/students-offered-an-ai-tutor-got-lower-grades-and-disengaged-from-course-materials-university-of-maryland-trial-finds · EdWorkingPaper https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1598.pdf
