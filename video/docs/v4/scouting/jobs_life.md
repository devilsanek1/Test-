## 1. You commute to the office... to sit on Zoom

**News.** Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report no. 1211, "Remote Work from the Office", by Natalia Emanuel
(NY Fed) and Emma Harrington (University of Virginia), dated October 2026. Covered by Forbes on 2026-10-27.
Survey of 1,835 full-time, college-educated US workers (Prolific; 2,422 before quality checks), plus admin data from a
Fortune 500 company and US time-use data (ATUS).

**Key numbers (from the report itself):**
- On their most recent office day, **70.6 %** were missing at least one teammate (on average 34.3 % of the team was absent).
- **48.8 %** had no in-person meetings at all; more than a third had neither in-person nor hybrid meetings.
- **37 %** had at least one meeting that was virtual *even though everyone in it was in the same office that day*.
- **15.1 %** had no casual face-to-face conversation with anyone.
- Time-use data: an office day had 7.1 h with colleagues before the pandemic, 6.3 h in 2026.
- Measures that count only work-from-home miss up to **three quarters** of real remote work.
- Main cause: "organizational sprawl" (teammates in other offices or fully remote), then non-compliance with office
  days, uncoordinated days, and taking Zoom calls instead of booking a room.

**Sources**
- NY Fed, Staff Report 1211 (Oct 2026): https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr1211 (PDF: https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1211.pdf, DOI https://doi.org/10.59576/sr.1211)
- Forbes, Caroline Castrillon, 2026-10-27: https://www.forbes.com/sites/carolinecastrillon/2026/10/27/why-return-to-office-mandates-fall-short-according-to-new-research/
- The Atlantic, "The Rise of In-Office Remote Work" (via Google News; same survey; date not confirmed)
- Context: same authors' book *In Person* (published 2026-10-13).

**Big question.** Is going back to the office actually worth the commute?
**So what?** Companies order people back for face-to-face time -> but teams are spread across cities and days don't line up
-> so the meeting goes on Zoom anyway -> so millions of people spend an hour+ and real money commuting for "a new background
for their Zoom calls" (the authors' words) -> the fix isn't more office days, it's putting teams in the same place on the same days.
**Thesis.** Everyone is talking about return-to-office mandates, but this new NY Fed survey reveals that half of office days
contain no in-person meeting at all, which matters because people are paying the commute for something the office isn't delivering.
**Frame-0 text:** "Half of office days: zero real meetings" (alt: "You commuted. The meeting was on Zoom.")
**Hooks**
1. "You got up at six, sat on a train for an hour... to join a Zoom call. From your desk. In the office."
2. "More than a third of people in the office had a video call with someone sitting in the same building."
3. "Your boss wants you back for face-to-face time. A New York Fed study checked if that's actually happening."

**Twist.** 37 % had a virtual meeting where every participant was in the same office. The cause is mostly not lazy
workers: it's that one remote teammate pulls the whole meeting online.
**Real person.** Natalia Emanuel & Emma Harrington (authors), in the report: "These workers commute in just to work remotely
from the office." / "many workers' commutes are simply yielding a new background for their Zoom calls." Ironic support:
Zoom CEO Eric Yuan (2023, quoted in the paper), calling staff back: "On Zoom, it's really hard."
**Share reason.** Everyone with an office job has lived this; you send it to the colleague you sit next to on calls, or to your boss.
**Key image.** A huge open-plan office (dense, alive: plants, coffee, a hundred tiny desks), camera pushes into one cozy desk
where a little person wears headphones talking to a grid of faces on screen, and one of those faces is sitting three desks away.
Scale: pull back to a city of trains all carrying people to their laptops.
**Evidence.** Strong: primary NY Fed report with exact numbers; one survey (online panel, college-educated only) plus admin
and time-use data that point the same way. Preliminary staff report, not peer-reviewed. Views are the authors', not the Fed's.
**Risks.** Don't say "the Fed says offices are pointless" (it's a staff report; it says the office under-delivers, not that
remote is better; the same authors argue in-person work matters). US, college-educated workers only. Avoid making bosses villains.

## 2. The first job is disappearing (UK): the bottom rung of the ladder

**News.** UK youth jobs crunch, one day before the Autumn Budget (2026-10-28).
- Learning and Work Institute briefing, 2026-10-20: **1.28 million** young people not in work or full-time education
  (highest since 2013; peak 1.35 m in Jan-Mar 2026). Hospitality and retail employee numbers **fell 117,000** in a year,
  more than all other industries combined. Vacancies 691,000 (lowest in over a decade); 5.3 people wanting a job per vacancy.
- House of Commons Library briefing, 2026-10-20: 715,000 unemployed 16-24s in Jun-Aug 2026 (+54,000 y/y), rate 15.6 % (from 14.6 %).
- CBI/Pertemps Employment Trends Survey (682 firms), reported 2026-10-12/13: 44 % say rising hiring/onboarding costs push them to
  experienced staff; 53 % offer fewer apprenticeships than they'd like; hiring confidence 15 % (from 24 %).
- 2026-10-15: Government (PM Andy Burnham, Work & Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden) + Fuller's, Mitchells & Butlers, Nando's,
  Wagamama, Burger King, Premier Inn pledge up to 100,000 paid placements (up to 4 weeks) for NEET 18-24s over three years.
  UKHospitality: sector lost ~100,000 jobs in two years and provided 31 % of first jobs for 16-24s (reported by The Drinks Business; not verified at source).
- Milburn review interim: 957,000 NEET at end-2025; **6 in 10 NEETs have never had a job (4 in 10 in 2005)**. Final report reportedly after the Budget.

**Sources**
- Learning and Work Institute, 2026-10-20: https://learningandwork.org.uk/labour-market-briefing-october-2026
- House of Commons Library SN05871, 2026-10-20: https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/SN05871/SN05871.pdf
- ITV News, 2026-10-12: https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-12/rising-labour-costs-threaten-job-opportunities-for-young-people-survey-finds
- Morning Advertiser, 2026-10-15: https://www.morningadvertiser.co.uk/Article/2026/10/15/hospitality-to-offer-100000-opportunities-for-young-people/
- The Drinks Business, Oct 2026: https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2026/10/pub-giants-pledge-100000-jobs-as-closures-threaten-thousands-more/
- FE Week on Milburn interim: https://feweek.co.uk/neet-numbers-could-hit-1-25m-within-five-years-milburn-warns/
- Low Pay Commission 2026 rates (18-20: GBP 10.85, +8.5 %; 21+: GBP 12.71): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69cbe06e024cdf09254f3f92/2026_Uprating_report_v2.pdf

**Big question.** Why is it so hard for young people to get a first job right now?
**So what?** The classic first job (Saturday shift in a pub, shop, café) is where you get "experience" -> those sectors cut
117,000 staff in a year as costs rose -> employers that do hire pick experienced people -> so more young people never get a first
job at all (6 in 10 NEETs never had one) -> and every entry-level ad still asks for experience: the ladder is missing its bottom rung.
**Thesis.** Everyone is talking about AI taking graduate jobs, but this month's UK data reveals the bigger hole is lower down:
the Saturday job in shops and pubs is vanishing, which matters because that's where you get the experience every other job asks for.
**Frame-0 text:** "The Saturday job is disappearing" (alt: "6 in 10 never had a job")
**Hooks**
1. "Every job ad wants experience. So where do you get it? For millions, it was a Saturday job. That's what's vanishing."
2. "Shops and pubs cut more jobs this year than every other industry in Britain combined."
3. "Six in ten young people out of work today have never had a job. In 2005 it was four in ten."

**Twist.** It's not mainly AI and not mainly grads: the loss is concentrated in the least glamorous jobs, the pub and shop
shifts that act as the training ground; and "never had a job" has gone from 4 in 10 to 6 in 10 NEETs.
**Real person.** Stephen Evans, CEO, Learning and Work Institute (2026-10-20): "around one million young people not in education,
employment or training". Pat McFadden, Work & Pensions Secretary: placements give a "first step on the ladder". Neil Davy, CEO
Family Business UK: "rising employment costs are making it harder for firms to take on staff."
(A named young person in their own words would be stronger; not found yet.)
**Share reason.** Parents send it to their teenager and vice versa; every young viewer has hit "need experience to get experience".
**Key image.** A ladder leaning against a tall glowing city of jobs, the bottom rung is a little pub/shop/café scene that is
being boarded up; tiny people crowd at the foot of the ladder, one jumping and not reaching. Cozy: one warm café counter with
"Saturday staff wanted" sign being taken down.
**Evidence.** Solid on numbers (ONS-based LWI and Commons Library; CBI survey). The *cause* is contested: employers blame
April 2025 employer NI rise and minimum wage increases; others point to a weak economy overall.
**Risks.** Politically live (Budget is tomorrow): must stay nonpartisan, present costs as "employers say". Note that under-21s
are exempt from employer NI, so do NOT claim NI is why teens aren't hired. UK-only (audience is international; frame it as a
UK case of a wider pattern only if sourced). Stats will move with the Budget and next ONS release (mid-Nov).

## 3. Phone bans in school: worse before they get better

**News.** Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro signed Senate Bill 1014 on Monday 2026-10-26 (reported 2026-10-27): a bell-to-bell
phone ban (including breaks and lunch) for public and private schools, policies required by the 2027-28 school year; more than
1.7 million K-12 students; about two dozen states already have bell-to-bell bans.
The best evidence on what happens next: Figlio & Özek, "The Impact of Cellphone Bans in Schools on Student Outcomes: Evidence
from Florida" (NBER w34388, 2025): in the first year **suspensions rose significantly** (especially for Black students); in the
**second year test scores improved modestly**; a big part of the gain came from **fewer unexcused absences**.

**Sources**
- WITF, 2026-10-27: https://www.witf.org/2026/10/27/shapiro-signs-bell-to-bell-cellphone-ban-for-pa-schools/
- NBER working paper w34388: https://nber.org/papers/w34388
- Chalkbeat, 2025-10-20: https://www.chalkbeat.org/2025/10/20/new-study-finds-cell-phone-ban-benefits-to-test-scores/
- The 74: https://www.the74million.org/article/school-cell-phone-bans-can-boost-test-scores/

**Big question.** Do school phone bans actually work?
**So what?** States race to ban phones -> the best study shows year one looks like failure (more suspensions, no learning
gain) -> year two, scores rise, mostly because kids skip school less -> so a district that judges its ban after one year
might scrap something that works; and the cost in suspensions falls unevenly.
**Thesis.** Everyone is talking about banning phones in school, but the best evidence reveals the ban looks like a failure in
year one and pays off in year two, largely by getting kids to show up, which matters because your kid's school may judge it too early.
**Frame-0 text:** "Phone bans: worse first, then better"
**Hooks**
1. "Pennsylvania just banned phones in school, all day, even at lunch. Florida did it first. Year one went badly."
2. "The first year of a phone ban, suspensions went up. Then something nobody expected happened."
3. "The phone ban did raise test scores. But not the way you'd think."

**Twist.** The gains came largely through attendance (fewer unexcused absences), not just "less distraction in class";
and the first year looked worse.
**Real person.** Christie Besack, teacher, Central Bucks High School West (WITF): "We've seen in our district that this works."
David Figlio (study author, Chalkbeat): "Black students seem to be accruing fewer of the benefits of the cellphone ban and more
of the disciplinary costs."
**Share reason.** Every parent and teacher has an opinion; it's a "send to the group chat" topic with a non-obvious answer.
**Key image.** A school hallway at dense scale (lockers, crowds, a wall of tiny glowing screens), then a cozy classroom where a
locked phone pouch hangs by the door; a two-panel "year 1 / year 2" season change on the same school building.
**Evidence.** News is solid (bill signed, two dozen states). Mechanism rests on one well-designed but single-district working
paper; other studies are mixed. Must say "one large study".
**Risks.** Phone bans are a heavily covered topic (lower surprise unless the year-1/year-2 twist leads). Racial disparity in
suspensions is sensitive; report it plainly or not at all. US-centric. Freshness of the mechanism study is 2025; the news hook is fresh.
