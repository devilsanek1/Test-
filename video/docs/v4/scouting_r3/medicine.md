## A. The first person treated with a virus designed by AI, aimed at a superbug nothing else could kill (2026-10-20)
- **What happened (2026-10-20):** Radical Numerics, a San Francisco AI-for-biology start-up (CEO Eric Nguyen, who came from the Arc Institute team behind the Evo DNA model), announced that a patient with a deadly E. coli infection that had resisted antibiotics AND every available phage received a phage cocktail containing variants designed by its AI model Omnii (Omnii-1), under a physician-led compassionate-use pathway; the company says the FDA authorized it. Phages are viruses that infect only bacteria; T7 infects only E. coli.
- **What AI did / didn't do:** AI did NOT invent a virus from scratch: it redesigned the "legs" (tail fibres) of a well-known natural phage, T7, so they grip this patient's resistant strain; the lab built the DNA, grew the phages, ran safety tests. In the dish, the cocktail "eliminated the patient's antibiotic-resistant isolate in vitro". In the patient: outcome NOT disclosed (privacy); phage therapy takes days to weeks and relapse is watched for months. Patient, hospital and country withheld. Stage: one patient, compassionate use (not a trial, not approved); company technical report, NOT peer reviewed ("We intend to publish ... through peer-reviewed research").
- **Sources:** company blog/technical report, 2026-10-20, https://www.radicalnumerics.ai/blog/omnii-phage · The New York Times, 2026-10-20 (read via its Boston Globe reprint, same day: https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/20/nation/startup-claims-use-ai-created-viruses-treat-patient/) · The Register, 2026-10-22, https://www.theregister.com/science/2026/10/22/human-receives-ai-designed-virus-for-the-first-time/5304874 · also Let's Data Science, Magica (secondary).
- **So what for an ordinary person, and when:** drug-resistant infections kill over a million people a year (Semafor, 2026-10-28); phage therapy exists, but finding a matching phage for one patient's bug "is remarkably time-consuming" (Azad). If AI can redesign a phage to fit in days, a hospital could one day order a made-to-measure bug-killer the way it orders a blood test. When: years, not months: this is n = 1; real trials and peer review must come first.
- **Why it's hopeful:** the scariest-sounding thing (a virus designed by AI) is aimed at the thing that is actually scary (a superbug); the lab-dish result is real; it was tried because antibiotics and every known phage had already failed.
- **Real people, own words:** Eric Nguyen (CEO, Radical Numerics) on tailor-made phages: "We think we can get it done in days." · Dr. Marisa Azad (infectious-disease clinician, The Ottawa Hospital, working with the company): "Hunting for phages is remarkably time-consuming." · Michael Baym (Harvard microbiologist, not involved): "If it works, that's incredible," and "My impulse is to be skeptical." (all via the NYT)
- **Frame-0 line:** "Doctors just gave someone an AI-made virus." (alt, question: "Would you take an AI-designed virus?")
- **Twist:** the virus is the medicine: it attacks only bacteria, not human cells; and nobody yet knows if it worked, which is exactly how real medicine starts.
- **Drawable key image:** a tiny lunar-lander phage, its six legs redrawn in a fresh pen colour, landing on a fat E. coli bacterium that has shrugged off a pile of pills.
- **Evidence strength and doubts:** MEDIUM-LOW for the claim, HIGH for the newsworthiness. The core event rests on the company's own word (no named hospital, no outcome, no peer review), confirmed only as "the company says" by NYT and The Register; outside experts are "wait and see". Must be told as "a company says" plus "we don't know yet". Risk: an unknown outcome is a soft ending for a "positive" brief, and "AI virus" can read as scary.

## C. The AI that cracked hard medical mysteries more than twice as often as doctors, now peer-reviewed in Science (2026-10-22)
- **What happened (2026-10-22):** Science published Microsoft AI's "Sequential diagnosis with language models" (Nori et al.; co-authors include Mustafa Suleyman, Dominic King, Eric Horvitz; publication date confirmed in Europe PMC). Its system, the MAI Diagnostic Orchestrator (DxO), makes AI models work like a panel of doctors: ask the patient questions, order tests one at a time (each with a price), then name the diagnosis. Tested on 304 complex published New England Journal of Medicine case records and 325 emergency-admission records, turned into step-by-step "virtual patients".
- **What AI did / didn't do:** DxO got 83.2 % of the complex cases right vs 36.1 % for generalist doctors (primary-care doctors and general internists working alone, with reference materials); 80.3 % vs 46.8 % on the emergency cases. It asked more questions before ordering tests, which kept the bill down (GPT-5 alone: 78.9 % at ~$8,400 of tests per case; DxO about $3,000 cheaper and ~4 points better). It did NOT see a single real patient: a simulation "under idealized information conditions"; no specialists or teams in the comparison; most of the gain comes from the underlying models, not Microsoft's wrapper; little gain on the emergency cases. Stage: peer-reviewed research; not deployed. Next: a study with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston) comparing DxO's suggestions with doctors' decisions for consenting patients, alongside normal care (no date).
- **Sources:** Science, 2026-10-22, DOI 10.1126/science.aeh7930 (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeh7930) · TechCrunch, 2026-10-22, https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/22/microsofts-mustafa-suleyman-says-its-ai-outperforms-doctors-on-hard-cases-as-he-chases-medical-superintelligence · Medical Xpress, 2026-10-23, https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-10-ai-generalist-doctors-medical.html · counterpoint in the same week: Nature news 2026-10-21 "AI doctors' orders come with a sky-high bill" (DOI 10.1038/d41586-026-03320-8).
- **So what for an ordinary person, and when:** many people wait years for the right name for what's wrong with them; an AI "second opinion" that reasons like a team of specialists could reach any doctor's office. When: not yet; the first real-patient comparison (Beth Israel) is only starting; Microsoft talks of 2027 for medical-record breakthroughs.
- **Why it's hopeful:** the scarce thing in medicine is expert attention ("the growing mismatch between demand and supply of medical expertise", King); this is a peer-reviewed sign that it can be copied; and it was cheaper, not pricier.
- **Real people, own words:** Mustafa Suleyman (CEO, Microsoft AI): "This is the kind of technology that everybody in the world wants." and "We're just true believers." · Dominic King (VP Health, Microsoft AI): "There is something very substantial and exciting here." (TechCrunch)
- **Frame-0 line:** "Doctors: 36 %. This AI: 83 %." (alt, question: "Would you let AI diagnose you?")
- **Twist:** the AI didn't win by knowing more facts, it won by acting like a careful team: asking first, testing second, and arguing with itself; and the doctors it beat were working alone, which is exactly the problem it could fix.
- **Drawable key image:** a scoreboard over a patient file: a lone doctor's tally at 36, a round table of five little AI "doctors" passing one file between them at 83.
- **Evidence strength and doubts:** HIGH that it's real and peer-reviewed (Science); MEDIUM on what it means: company-run, simulated cases, generalists only, no patient helped yet. "Medical superintelligence" is Microsoft's hype word: don't repeat it as fact. "AI beats doctors" can read as a threat, so the positive frame must be "a second opinion for everyone", stated with the limits. Not the owner's first image ("finding medication"), closer to "solving disease".

## D. AI spots pancreatic cancer on "normal" CT scans 475 days before diagnosis (back in the news 2026-10-26; paper online 2026-04-28)
- **What happened:** Mayo Clinic's REDMOD (Radiomics-based Early Detection MODel) reads the fine texture of the pancreas on ordinary abdominal CT scans. BMJ re-released the study on 2026-10-26 (ScienceDaily), but the paper itself, Mukherjee ... Chari, Goenka, "Next-generation AI for visually occult pancreatic cancer detection ...", Gut, went online 2026-04-28 (Europe PMC), with Mayo's own release then. So the buzz is fresh, the science is six months old.
- **What AI did / didn't do:** on CT scans from 219 people that radiologists had read as clean but who were diagnosed later, plus 1,243 people who stayed cancer-free, it flagged the hidden cancer an average of 475 days (~16 months) before diagnosis: 73 % caught vs 39 % for experienced radiologists; for scans more than 2 years before diagnosis, 68 % vs 23 %. Independent check: ~81 % of cancer-free scans called correctly (539 patients; 87.5 % on an NIH set), so about 1 in 5 healthy scans wrongly flagged (1 in 8 on the NIH set); repeat scans agree 90-92 % of the time. It did NOT find a single cancer in a living patient in real time: retrospective (old scans), a study population that lacked ethnic diversity, prospective validation still needed (high-risk people: new-onset diabetes, unexplained weight loss). Not in clinics; no date given. Peer-reviewed.
- **Sources:** ScienceDaily / BMJ Group, 2026-10-26, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261024222031.htm · Gut, DOI 10.1136/gutjnl-2025-337266 (online 2026-04-28, Europe PMC) · Mayo Clinic News Network (April 2026), https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/mayo-clinic-ai-detects-pancreatic-cancer-up-to-3-years-before-diagnosis-in-landmark-validation-study/ (blocked for our fetcher; date from a search snippet).
- **So what for an ordinary person, and when:** pancreatic cancer is usually found too late; millions of people already get abdominal CT scans for other reasons, so the warning could come free from a scan they already had. When: after prospective trials, years away.
- **Why it's hopeful:** the cancer was already visible, just not to us; "the timing of diagnosis is the single most critical determinant of survival outcomes" (the authors).
- **Real person, own words:** Dr. Ajit Goenka (Mayo Clinic radiologist, senior author), at the April launch: "The greatest barrier to saving lives from pancreatic cancer has been our inability to see the disease" and "This AI can now identify the signature of cancer from a normal-appearing pancreas" (Decrypt, 2026-04-29, https://decrypt.co/366095/mayo-clinic-says-ai-can-detect-pancreatic-cancer-years-before-human-doctors); Mayo's release also: "more than 85% of patients receive a diagnosis after the cancer has already spread" (as quoted by Decrypt). The October release names no one. Next step named by Mayo: AI-PACED, a prospective study of people at raised risk (e.g. new-onset diabetes) with repeat AI-read CT scans; no enrolment numbers or dates found.
- **Frame-0 line:** "This scan was 'normal'. It wasn't."
- **Twist:** nobody missed anything a human could see: the cancer was in the texture, and the AI read it 16 months early.
- **Drawable key image:** a grey CT slice with the pancreas drawn in; a calendar peeling back 475 days; a faint speckled patch that lights up warm.
- **Evidence strength and doubts:** MEDIUM-HIGH science (peer-reviewed, external validation) but retrospective, with about 1 in 5 healthy scans wrongly flagged, which would matter in screening; freshness weak (an April paper re-released); the owner asked for "very recent".

## E. 37,000 AI agents read 56,000 drug trials and found which medicines tend to work (Science, 2026-10-22)
- **What happened (2026-10-22):** Stanford's James Zou lab (Zhang, Eckmann, Miao, Mahon, Zou; Department of Biomedical Data Science) published "The Virtual Biotech: A multi-agent AI framework for therapeutic discovery and development" in Science (date confirmed in Europe PMC): an organisation of AI agents built like a drug company, with "divisions" for target discovery, safety, choosing the type of drug, and clinical development.
- **What AI did / didn't do:** more than 37,000 agents read and annotated the outcomes of 55,984 clinical trials and found that drugs aimed at genes switched on in only specific cell types were 48 % more likely to reach the market, with 32 % fewer adverse events. It also proposed a strategy for a lung-cancer treatment and worked out likely reasons a stopped ulcerative-colitis trial failed. It did NOT make, test or give anyone a drug; the 48 % is a pattern in past trials (correlation, not proof); everything is "human-guided". Stage: peer-reviewed research.
- **Sources:** Science, 2026-10-22, DOI 10.1126/science.aeg6779 (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeg6779; abstract via Europe PMC). Press coverage: not found within the search budget (single primary source).
- **So what for an ordinary person, and when:** most of what drug companies spend goes on medicines that fail late; a rule that picks likelier winners earlier could mean more medicines, sooner and safer. When: indirect, years; it changes which drugs get made, not your prescription.
- **Why it's hopeful:** AI did the reading no human team has time for, and the lesson is simple and testable.
- **Real person, own words:** none found (James Zou is the senior author).
- **Frame-0 line:** "37,000 AIs just read 56,000 drug trials."
- **Twist:** the AI's big discovery isn't a molecule, it's a rule of thumb hiding in our own records: aim at one kind of cell.
- **Drawable key image:** a tiny office building full of little agent-figures with badges (Targets, Safety, Trials) passing folders off a mountain of trial files.
- **Evidence strength and doubts:** HIGH that it's published (Science); the finding is correlational; abstract, no patient, no person, no verified buzz. A reserve, not a lead.
