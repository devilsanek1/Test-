## A. Nobody got paid to fill the tanks (Europe's gas)

Fact status (2026-10-27 check): numbers and quotes verified at source; frame 0/hook true as worded; remaining risks are the spread-vs-war framing and wholesale-to-bill lag (see Uncertain). Unverified quote replaced.

**Big question.** Will heating my home cost more this winter, and why?

**So-what chain.** Europe's gas storage is the lowest for late October since records began, so what? Gas storage only works
because summer gas is normally cheaper than winter gas: traders buy in summer, store it and get paid in winter. So what?
For much of 2026 summer gas cost about the same as, or more than, winter gas (at the Dutch TTF hub), so storing it lost money and the tanks filled slowly.
So what? With less in storage and LNG from the Gulf disrupted, wholesale gas prices are about 3x January's, and Euronews estimates a typical gas-heated home
(10,000 kWh, Nov-Mar) could pay about EUR 600 more, about EUR 715 with VAT, more in a cold winter. So what? One degree lower on
the thermostat could cut energy bills by up to 10% (IEEFA's Jaller-Makarewicz, in Euronews), the one lever a household controls.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Gas is expensive again, it's the war, nothing I can do."
- During: "Wait, the tanks are low because filling them LOST money?"
- After: "Oh, storage runs on a price gap between summer and winter. This year the gap flipped, so nobody had a reason to fill up, and my bill pays for it."
- Tell-a-friend version: "Europe's gas tanks are the emptiest on record because this summer gas cost more than winter gas, so storing it lost money. That's why your heating could cost hundreds more."

**Facts, sources, dates.**

EUR 600 / 715 household estimate. Source: Euronews (Piero Cingari), 2026-10-27, euronews.com/2026/10/27/european-natural-gas-prices-soar-what-it-means-for-your-winter-bills.
- Lowest for the date since records began (2011); thermostat tip: IEEFA, Oct 2026, ieefa.org/resources/eu-may-need-cut-winter-gas-demand-7-gas-storage-record-seasonal-low.
- Inverted / flat summer-winter spread: Timera Energy 2026 (timera-energy.com/blog/inverted-seasonal-spreads-complicate-2026-outlook-for-storage-operators/); ABN AMRO research, summer 2026; Newsbase, Aug 2026 (summer at a EUR 0.91-1.05/MWh premium).
- Wood Mackenzie expects storage of 17-20% by end of March. Qatari LNG largely absent (Hormuz disruption, force majeure extended into Oct; exact "since May" not confirmed in Oct reporting). EU bans Russian LNG from Jan 2027.
- Fact-check 2026-10-27: Euronews piece re-opened and confirmed (8:43 GMT+1); IEEFA's record-low analysis itself dates from 9 Oct (72.4% then), Euronews 27 Oct carries it forward. Timera (26 Jan 2026): S26 traded >EUR 0.5/MWh above W26; also notes inversions in 3 of the past 4 years, and 2023 still reached 98.5%, so the spread is one big reason, not the only one.
- Background check (this doc): Euronews 2026-08-20 and 08-26 confirm the summer surge (TTF ~EUR 64 in Aug, storage 63% on 24 Aug vs 79% five-year average); a Guardian report confirms "lowest gas reserves in 13 years".

**Uncertain or disputed.**
- RESOLVED: the 27 Oct Euronews piece exists and matches every figure above. The EUR 700 stays "could be about", attributed to Euronews (6 ct/kWh gap x 10,000 kWh, Nov-Mar, plus 20% VAT).
- The spread is not independent of the war: the Gulf LNG loss made prompt summer gas scarce, which is what inverted the curve. Say the war is why, and the flipped price is how it reached the tanks; never imply traders alone chose not to fill (states also set fill targets, cut from 90% to 80%).
- Analysts describe the spread differently ("inverted" vs "too flat to cover storage costs"). Say "summer gas cost about as much as winter gas, or more".
- The bill effect is wholesale; fixed contracts and price caps delay or soften it, and pass-through speed differs by country (Euronews, Aug: fast in NL, nearly a year in DE/AT). Weather decides a lot.
- Storage % moves daily; re-check GIE AGSI+ the day the script locks.

**Fairness notes.** No villains: not traders, not governments; it's a market mechanism. Don't say "shortage" (EU Gas
Coordination Group saw no immediate supply risk, 3 Sep). Mention the war and Hormuz as context in one clause, no blame.
Europe-only: say "in Europe" on frame 0 or line 1.

## B. Commute to Zoom (the office you travel to for a video call)

Fact status (2026-10-27 check): every figure and quote verified in the NY Fed PDF; frame 0 softened to "no face-to-face meetings"; 37% twist de-conflated; Forbes coverage unconfirmed.

**Big question.** Is going back to the office actually worth the commute?

**So-what chain.** Companies order staff back for face-to-face time, so what? A new NY Fed study finds that on their last office day, about half of
people (48.8%) had no in-person meeting at all. So what? Mostly because teammates sit in other offices or cities,
or come in on different days, so the meeting goes on Zoom anyway; and even with everyone in, 37% still had a video call. So what? People pay the commute, time and money, for, in the authors' words, "a new background
for their Zoom calls". So what? The fix isn't more office days; it's the same people in the same place on the same days.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Return-to-office is bosses vs workers. Either the office matters or it doesn't."
- During: "Wait, a third of people had a video call with someone in the same building?"
- After: "Oh, it's a coordination problem. One remote teammate pulls everyone onto Zoom, so the office doesn't deliver the thing it's for."
- Tell-a-friend version: "A New York Fed study found half of office days have no face-to-face meeting, mostly because if one teammate is elsewhere, everyone ends up on Zoom. So the commute buys you a new background."

## C. The padlock key was 4

Fact status (2026-10-27 check): core facts verified across 5 outlets; payoff fixed (blocks were automatic, within hours); CVE removed; 10 days old.

**Big question.** What does the padlock in my browser actually mean?

**So-what chain.** Eight secret keys at eMudhra were tiny numbers, one literally "4", so what? eMudhra is one of a few dozen
companies your browser trusts to vouch that a website is who it says it is. So what? Any one of them can vouch for ANY
site, so with those keys someone could have made a "secure", padlocked fake of almost any website, your bank's included,
for about 18 months (Helme's analysis). So what? Nobody is known to have done it, and updated browsers now block the keys,
but the padlock is only as strong as the weakest vouching company, and background updates are what actually protect you.

**Understanding shift.**
- Before: "Padlock means the site is safe."
- During: "Wait, the key was the number FOUR?"
- After: "Oh, the padlock means some company I've never heard of vouches for the site, and dozens of them can vouch for anything. Updating my browser is how bad vouchers get cut off."
- Tell-a-friend version: "The padlock just means a company vouches for the site, and one of those companies had a secret key that was literally the number 4. Browsers caught it, which is why updates matter."

|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | Hook | "The secret behind that little padlock is meant to be a number hundreds of digits long. One of them was four." | Frame 0: "The lock on your bank's website was '4'"; a giant padlock drawn in ink, its dial clicks to a single "4". |
| 3-9 s | Stakes | "And that one could have let someone build a padlocked fake of almost any website. Your bank's included." | A street of shop-front websites, each with a padlock; one bank door has a perfect twin across the road. |

| 32-40 s | Payoff | "Nobody's known to have used them, and browsers blocked them within hours, quietly, in the background. That's why you leave automatic updates on." | A phone on a bedside table: a small "Updated" tick appears on its own; the twin bank door across the street fades; hold. |

**Real person.** Scott Helme, security researcher: "One of eMudhra's certificate authority private keys is the number 4"
(verbatim, as quoted by CoinDesk, 2026-10-21).

**Facts, sources, dates.**
- 17 Oct 2026 report (researcher "Wayne", via the Chrome Root Program); Chrome (CRLSet) and Firefox (OneCRL) blocked on 17 Oct, reaching default-settings users in under ~3 h; Apple 19 Oct; Microsoft 20 Oct; Go, Node.js, Python certifi and Rust webpki-roots shipped fixes (developer tools need an actual update). Six intermediates were TLS, two code-signing. Google also plans to drop a parent root. (Scout's CVE-2026-111943 not found in any checked source; don't use.) Sources: CoinDesk 2026-10-21 (coindesk.com/tech/2026/10/21/a-company-that-vouches-for-websites-had-secret-keys-so-weak-that-8-of-them-were-cracked); Cloudflare blog (blog.cloudflare.com/emudhra-certificate-compromise/); BleepingComputer; The Hacker News 2026-10.
- Background check (this doc): The Next Web, CSO Online, The Register, GovInfoSecurity confirm "the number 4", all eight keys recovered from public certificates in under a second, the browser blocks, and eMudhra's statement that it will stop issuing SSL/TLS certificates (its Indian eSign business unaffected); eMudhra blames a flawed key-generation process.

**Uncertain or disputed.**
- Attribution: a researcher named "Wayne" reported it; Helme recovered the keys independently. Say "a security researcher found..." and quote Helme.
- One key is reported as 237 or 247 in different outlets; use only 4, 56 and 115.
- The 18-month window is Helme's analysis (keys generated April 2025); eMudhra says most of the intermediates were never used in production (one outlet: one was). Keep "could have", never "did"; "almost any website" rests on Helme's forged-certificate test, attribute it.
- Exclusion check: one outlet says Helme credited an AI model (Claude) with recovering the keys. That's single-source and not the story; leave it out entirely, so Anthropic is not an entity in this video.

**Fairness notes.** Near miss, no victims, fixed in days: never say anyone faked a bank site. eMudhra is not a cartoon
villain; the story is the trust system (any voucher vouches for anything). Keep India out of the framing beyond the
company name.
