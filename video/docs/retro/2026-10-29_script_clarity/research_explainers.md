## Sources

(collected so far; the findings above cite them by number once written)

- S1. Olson, R. (2015) *Houston, We Have a Narrative* (U. Chicago Press); NCSE on the ABT and South Park's rule
  ("replacing and's with either but's or therefore"): https://ncse.ngo/and-therefore-randy-olson-and-art-science-storytelling-part-1 (practitioner; book by a scientist-filmmaker).
- S2. Loewenstein, G. (1994) "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation", *Psychological Bulletin*
  116(1) 75-98: https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/ (study: a review).
- S3. Bullock, Colón Amill, Shulman, Dixon (2019) "Jargon as a barrier to effective science communication",
  *Public Understanding of Science* 28(7): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31354058/ ; full text:
  https://comm.osu.edu/sites/comm.osu.edu/files/PUS%202019-%20Bullock%20et%20al..pdf (study: N = 650, 2x2
  experiment; jargon lowered processing fluency, F(1,636) = 76.03, eta2 = .11; mouseover definitions had no effect on
  fluency, no interaction; lower fluency raised resistance to the message and risk perception, lowered support).
- S4. Heath, C. and Heath, D. (2007) *Made to Stick*; Newton's 1990 tappers study (tappers predicted 50 %, listeners
  got 3 of 120 songs, 2.5 %): https://law.temple.edu/aer/?p=2964 (study, reported via a practitioner book).
- S5. Somerville, R. and Hassol, S. J. (2011) "Communicating the science of climate change", *Physics Today* 64(10);
  its table of words the public hears differently ("aerosol" = "spray can", "positive feedback" = "praise"),
  reproduced: https://grist.org/climate-change/2011-11-22-the-art-of-talking-climate-science/ (style guide by
  scientists).
- S6. Rakedzon et al. (2017) "Automatic jargon identifier ..." (the De-Jargonizer), *PLOS ONE* 12(8) e0181742:
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5549884 (study: 98 % familiar words, so at most 2 % jargon, for a lay
  reader, after Hu and Nation; 95 % as the bare minimum, after Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski; lay summaries still
  had 8-12 %; flags words with a different everyday meaning as a known blind spot).
- S7. Muller, D. (2008) PhD thesis, U. Sydney, *Designing Effective Multimedia for Physics Education*; summary:
  https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx ; journal
  version: https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/6345 (study: clear expository videos felt
  clear and taught almost nothing; videos that first voiced the viewer's wrong idea taught more).
- S8. "How to Succeed in MrBeast Production" (internal guide, leaked 2024), text:
  https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/ ; context:
  https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/ (practitioner: "for 50 million people to
  understand something it must be simple"; "the first minute has the most loss"; "Stop telling people what they will
  be watching and start showing them").
- S9. YouTube Shorts' "viewed vs. swiped away" (Studio > Analytics > Content > Shorts), described by creator guides,
  e.g. https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics (platform metric, via
  practitioners; no official benchmark).
- S10. Gernsbacher, M. A. (2015) "Video Captions Benefit Everyone", *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain
  Sciences* 2(1): https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Video-Captions-Benefit-Everyone_2015.pdf
  (study: a review of 100+ studies; captions help comprehension, attention, memory, most for second-language viewers;
  critique: https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/video-captions-benefit-everyone-an-investigation/).
- S11. Glynn, Duit and Thiele (1995), Teaching-With-Analogies: introduce the target, recall the analogue, identify the
  relevant features, map the similarities, indicate where it breaks down, draw conclusions; "If ... the analogies are
  not well chosen or applied systematically, they may be ineffective or cause confusion":
  https://www.csun.edu/~vceed002/books/sourcebook/chapters/10-analogies/teaching-analogies.html (style guide from
  education research).
- S12. Rober, M., on ReThinking with Adam Grant (TED, 2024), transcript:
  https://www.ted.com/pages/how-mark-rober-hides-science-vegetables-in-viral-videos-transcript (practitioner: "you
  gotta hook 'em"; "No one shares a video they didn't finish watching"; "hiding the vegetables"); Stanford talk, 2025:
  https://stanforddaily.com/2025/05/19/youtuber-and-former-nasa-engineer-mark-rober-draws-full-crowd-speaks-to-scientific-storytelling/
  ("Focus on the feeling").
- S13. Kurzgesagt, "Can You Trust Kurzgesagt Videos?" (their process: books and papers, then experts; "being brief
  while not simplifying too much"), transcript: https://voicetube.com/videos/73977 ; about page:
  https://kurzgesagt.org/about/ (practitioner).
- S14. Dahlstrom, M. F. (2014) "Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences",
  *PNAS* 111 (Suppl. 4) 13614-13620: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170 (study: a review; narratives
  are easier to understand and more engaging than "logical-scientific" lists).
- S15. Haviland, S. E. and Clark, H. H. (1974) "What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in comprehension",
  *J. Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13; Clark (1977) "Bridging":
  https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_Bridging_%20reprint%201977.pdf (study: "the beer was warm"
  is read faster after "we got some beer out of the trunk" than after "we checked the picnic supplies": a "the X"
  with no earlier X costs the listener a bridge).
- S16. McGraw, A. P. and Warren, C. (2010) "Benign violations: making immoral behavior funny", *Psychological Science*
  21(8): https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf (study: a joke lands only when the
  "wrong" thing is also felt as harmless, both at once).
- S17. Sarcasm and irony in a second language: Dolan (BYU) https://schwa.byu.edu/files/2016/01/Dolan_Sarcasm.pdf
  (ESL listeners less accurate on sarcastic context and tone); Cheang and Pell (2011), *Pragmatics and Cognition*
  19(2): https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pc.19.2.02che (sarcasm in an unfamiliar language
  recognised near chance) (studies, small).
- S18. Yeo, Cacciatore et al. on humour in science posts (JCOM, *Public Understanding of Science* 2021): mirth raised
  the communicator's likability and the wish to comment and follow, with effects that vary by what people already
  know: https://jcom.sissa.it/author/sara-k-yeo ; project:
  https://informalscience.org/project/exploring-effectiveness-using-humor-communicating-about-science/ (studies).
- S19. A news explainer naming the object for lay readers: Technical.ly, 16 Jun 2025, "servers, or computers that are
  designed to store and process data": https://technical.ly/civics/what-are-data-centers-explainer.md (example).
