## Sources gathered so far (checked)

- Alison MacAdam, NPR Training, "The journey from print to radio storytelling" (2017, re-posted 2025):
  https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape
  ("our ears can only handle one fact or idea per sentence"; "keep subjects cozy with their verbs"; dependent clauses
  "toxic to radio writing"; numbers make listeners do math; deliberate transitions). Style guide.
- Alison MacAdam, NPR Training, "How to edit with your ears":
  https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/28/g-s1-67202/how-to-edit-with-your-ears ("once a listener gets
  confused, the forward momentum of the information outpaces their ability to comprehend"; "Listeners only have one
  chance to hear a story"). Style guide.
- NPR Life Kit / Student Podcast Challenge, "7 tips" (2023):
  https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice ("like you're telling a story to
  a friend"; "the vehicle" vs "the car"). Practitioner.
- Mervin Block, top tips, from Writing Broadcast News: Shorter, Sharper, Stronger (2nd ed., 1998):
  https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0073511935/student_view0/nrw_plus/chapter9/top_tips_of_the_trade.html
  (S-V-O; one idea a sentence; familiar words in familiar combinations; stress word at the end; "Don't raise
  questions you don't answer"; read it aloud). Practitioner (the standard broadcast text).
- Chris Wodskou, CBC Radio, "The craft of writing for radio: 16 tips" (2016):
  https://www.cbc.ca/radio/docproject/blog/the-craft-of-writing-for-radio-16-tips-from-a-veteran-script-writer-1.3562474
  (conversational = "speaking to you as a familiar"; concrete images; too many numbers lose people; plays on words
  "cheesy" when there to look clever). Practitioner.
- Haviland and Clark 1974, "What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in comprehension", Journal of Verbal
  Learning and Verbal Behavior 13(5): 512-521, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4 (checked via Crossref).
  Study.
- Christianson, Hollingworth, Halliwell and Ferreira 2001, "Thematic roles assigned along the garden path linger",
  Cognitive Psychology 42: 368-407,
  https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf
  (a first wrong reading persists; comprehension aims at "good enough"). Study.
- Haviland and Clark 1974, primary PDF (Herbert Clark's Stanford page):
  https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Haviland,%20S.E.%20_%20Clark,%20H.H.%20_What's%20new_%20Acquiring%20new%20information%20as%20a%20process%20in%20comprehension_%201974.pdf
  ("We got some beer out of the trunk. The beer was warm." understood in 835 ms; "We checked the picnic supplies. The
  beer was warm." in 1016 ms: a "the X" with no direct antecedent costs a bridging step). Study.
- Gordon, Grosz and Gilliom 1993, "Pronouns, names, and the centering of attention in discourse", Cognitive Science
  17: 311-347, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1703_1 (pronouns beat a repeated name only when the referent is the
  prominent one, the subject/center). Study.
- Arnold, Eisenband, Brown-Schmidt and Trueswell 2000, "The rapid use of gender information", Cognition 76: B13-B26,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10822045/ (LISTENERS, eye-tracking: gender and accessibility, i.e. the
  first-mentioned character, both pick a pronoun's referent ~200 ms after it). Study.
- Reber and Schwarz 1999, "Effects of perceptual fluency on judgments of truth", Consciousness and Cognition 8:
  338-342, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10487787/ (easier-to-read statements judged true more often). Study.
- Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman 2004, "Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure", PSPR 8: 364-382,
  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3 (easy to process = liked). Study (review).
- Alter and Oppenheimer 2009, "Uniting the tribes of fluency", PSPR 13: 219-235,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19638628/ (fluent = judged more true, across many kinds of ease, including
  "phonologically simple"). Study (review).
- Oppenheimer 2006, "Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective of necessity", Applied Cognitive
  Psychology 20: 139-156, https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178 (needlessly complex words make the writer seem LESS
  intelligent). Study.
- Sadoski, Goetz and Rodriguez 2000, "Engaging texts: effects of concreteness on comprehensibility, interest, and
  recall", Journal of Educational Psychology 92: 85-95, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85 ; ERIC abstract
  https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ619358 ("Concreteness was overwhelmingly the best predictor of overall comprehensibility,
  interest, and recall"). Study. Also Sadoski et al. 1993, ERIC https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ463652 (familiarity and
  concreteness cause comprehensibility; all three cause interest). Study.
- Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber 1989, "The curse of knowledge in economic settings", Journal of Political Economy 97:
  1232-1254, https://doi.org/10.1086/261651 . Study.
- Newton 1990 (Stanford PhD), the tapping study, as reported in the BPS Psychologist's review of Pinker's The Sense of
  Style: https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/cursed-knowledge (tappers predicted 50 %, listeners got 2.5 %, 3 of
  120; Pinker: experts think in "chunks" the reader lacks, and in what a thing is FOR, not what it looks like). Study
  (reported) + practitioner (Pinker).
- Chip Heath and Dan Heath, "The Curse of Knowledge", HBR, Dec 2006: https://hbr.org/2006/12/the-curse-of-knowledge
  (an expert's abstract phrase summarizes "the wealth of concrete data in their heads", the listener hears "only
  opaque phrases"). Practitioner.
