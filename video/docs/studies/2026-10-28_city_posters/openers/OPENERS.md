# Openers: the "did you know" family, researched and scored (for video 5)

Asked by the owner, 2026-10-29 ~09:00 UTC (verbatim): "Research more of the similar openings in the reels TikTok and
score and pick the absolute best options". Then (relayed): "Even with did you know the core line is instantly more
interesting" and "I mean finish your analysis of more openings like maybe there is a better one". The model opener (the
owner's pick): the Big Bend reel, @civilext: "Did you know that New York once got a proposal to build this giant
2,000-foot tall bending skyscraper? It's called the Big Bend." Research only (one agent, max effort), 2026-10-29.

## Summary (one screen)

**The verdict.** No opener family beats "Did you know ..." by enough to drop it. As a family, "There's a [thing] in
[place] ..." edges it (8.4 against 8.0 on our scale), because its grammar puts a place and a thing in the first clause, while "Did you
know" alone is also the stamp of low-effort trivia channels (rows 33-34: a few hundred views). "Why does this ...?" ties
it (8.1). The best lines merge the two: **"Did you know there's a ..."**, which keeps the owner's friend's question and
adds the concrete picture. What matters more than the family is what the first line says. For video 5 the strongest
content is the orbit paradox (a computer overheating in freezing space): the biggest open loop, fresh news, one
impossible picture, and a ready-made comment fight ("space isn't cold, it's empty").

**The best 3 openers for video 5** (Part 3; spoken American English, with the line after it):
1. **"Did you know there's a computer in space right now that keeps overheating?"** Then: "Space is freezing!" (8.7, the
   "Did you know" family; core line 2 rewritten.) Why: the owner's model frame carrying the strongest paradox we have, in
   13 words; frame 0 is one picture (a satellite over the Earth's curve, icy blue, then glowing orange on "overheating").
2. **"Space is freezing. So why does this computer keep overheating?"** Then: "It's the size of a fridge." (8.4, the "why"
   family; core line 2 as written.) Why: the most front-loaded line ("freezing" at ~1.2 s, "overheating" at ~3.8 s), the
   Big Bend's own title pattern, and it doubles as the cover line.
3. **"Thirty meters under this park in Stockholm, there's a nuclear bunker full of computers."** Then: "And it gets hot
   down there." (8.4, the "There's a" family.) Why: the most concrete and the calmest; the words follow the camera (the
   park, down 30 m, the bunker, the servers); keeps the current spine's order. Its "Did you know" twin scores the same
   (Part 3, row 5).

**Core lines as "Did you know" openers** (the owner's note): core line 2 gains the most (8.3 as a literal rewrite, 8.7
rewritten as pick 1). Core line 1, "Did you know your chatbot could heat an apartment in Helsinki?", gets warmer (7.7)
but spends the payoff in second one: keep it as the last line, where the spine already has it. Core line 3 as an
opener (6.0) is a three-place list (an AI tell), and frame 0 can only show one picture.

**The 3 best patterns:** "There's a [thing] in [place] ..." (8.4); "Why does this [thing] ...?" (8.1); "Did you know
[place] has [odd thing]?" (8.0). Close behind: "What happens if ...?" (7.9). **Avoid:** a question or claim aimed at the
viewer's wallet, a secret, or an instruction (our v3, "Buying a laptop soon?": 64.5 % skipped, twice v1); generic
trivia with no picture; hype words; a list in line 1.

**One choice for the writer:** picks 1 and 2 open the film in orbit (then "so where do you put a machine this hot?":
the bunker, the mine, the sea, then Helsinki keeps the heat). Pick 3 keeps the bunker first. If the owner picks core
line 2, picks 1 and 2 are its natural openers; with core line 1, any pick works and core line 1 lands at the payoff.

**What the evidence says, in five lines:**
1. The first 3 s decide, and Instagram measures exactly that. Our v3 lost 60 points by 2 s.
2. Say what it is about at once, in plain words, over one clear picture.
3. The picture has to work with the sound off.
4. Curiosity comes from one specific gap, so the odd detail belongs in line 1. Volume and hype add nothing (brief §14-17).
5. "Did you know" is a frame, not a magic phrase. It works with one concrete, odd, visible thing and fails as generic
   trivia.

## Part 2: the patterns, scored
Each criterion 1-10; the mean is of the seven. **Natural** = a friend talking, not a presenter or an ad. **Curiosity**
= an open loop the viewer must close. **Concrete** = a place, a thing, a number in the first sentence. **Not pushy** =
no sell, no "you need to", no shouting. **Fits our look** = the first spoken line is what frame 0 shows (one
impossible picture), early enough that "the expected, then reality" can play by ~2-3 s. **Comments** = people argue or
add their own. **Evidence** = how well it is known to perform. Differences under ~0.3 are noise.

**Notes on the top patterns:**
- **P3** is how people start a story at dinner. Its grammar forces a place and a thing into the first clause, it asks
  the viewer nothing (the calmest), and the sentence is already a picture. Few big accounts lead with it, so its
  evidence is thinner.
- **P5** is the strongest open loop, and people answer it in the comments. Its concreteness lives in the picture ("this
  computer"), so it needs a strong frame 0. It also makes the best cover line (the Big Bend's own title).
- **P1** is the owner's model and the most natural question a friend asks. As a family its concreteness swings from 3
  to 10 with the content (rows 1-2 against 33-34). At its best it borrows P3's grammar: "Did you know there's a ...".
- P6 has the biggest single number (52M) and Rein's data, but a "what if" needs a setup picture before the impossible
  one. P4 is the most frame-0-ready but can sound like a presenter. P7 is our hook rule in words and has the best
  comment pull, but written lazily it becomes the "It's not X, it's Y" template (RULE #1).

**Patterns to avoid, and why:**
- **P10, the sell, the secret, the instruction** ("Buying a laptop soon?", "Apple doesn't want you to know", "Try
  this"). It reads as an ad or as bait. Our v3 is the case: 64.5 % skipped against v1's 32 %, 60 points gone by 2 s, no
  shares, saves or comments.
- **Generic "Did you know that [vague]?"** with no place or picture (rows 33-34): the phrase alone is a trivia-channel
  stamp.
- **Hype and shouting:** "You won't believe", "This will blow your mind", "Stop scrolling", "Nobody is talking about
  this" (brief §14-17; RULE #1's stock phrases).
- **A list in line 1** (three places at once): the rule of three is an AI tell, and frame 0 can only show one picture.
- **The turn as a template** ("but get this", "It's not X, it's Y"): keep the idea (the expected, then reality) and
  play it with the picture and plain words.
- **P9, first person**, is not wrong but does not fit us: we have no host on camera, so the "I" has no face.
