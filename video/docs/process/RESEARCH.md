# Research: how art direction writes principles as feelings and tools, never placements (2026-10-30)

**Status:** in progress (research started; findings fill in below as each source is checked).

**The question (BRIEF.md, the owner):** our guides mix feelings, tools, concrete placements and numbers; agents copy
placements (no imagination) or miss whole areas (incomplete frames). How do professionals state "a feel of distance"
and its tools (layers) so every artist invents the frame, and still nothing is forgotten?

**Strength key:** A = the author's own book or a studio's own account; B = a course or a talk by the practitioner;
C = a practitioner's article, interview or a secondary write-up.

## Findings

**F1. A feeling is made by a small set of visual components, each a dial between contrast and affinity (Block).**
- **The practice.** Bruce Block (*The Visual Story*, 3rd ed. 2020) says every picture is built from seven basic visual
  components: space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement and rhythm. The publisher describes the book as using them to
  "convey moods and emotions, create a visual style", and says "the connection between story and visual structures"
  guides the choices of camera, staging, lighting and editing. Each component runs from affinity (alike) to contrast
  (different): "Visual structure is based on an understanding of the Principle of Contrast & Affinity." Contrast in a
  component raises the visual intensity; affinity lowers it ("Contrast increases visual intensity, but affinity
  decreases visual intensity"). Space has four kinds (deep, flat, limited, ambiguous), and deep space is made by depth
  cues: perspective, size difference, relative movement, textural diffusion, aerial diffusion, shape change, tonal
  separation, colour separation, up/down position, overlap and focus. The book's own order: the components, then
  "Story & Visual Structures", then "Practice, Not Theory"; "picture makers must understand story structure or they
  may never be able to correctly use visual structure".
- **Source and strength.** A: the publisher's description and contents
  ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story-Creating-the-Visual-Structure-of-Film-TV-and-Digital-Media/Block/p/book/9781138014152));
  the two quoted lines of the book through the search index of the
  [O'Reilly chapter 2](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9780240807799/009_9780080551692_chapter2.html)
  and [chapter 9](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9781317745693/xhtml/Ch09.xhtml) previews (the
  pages themselves refused a direct read, HTTP 403). C: Z. Faulkes's
  [review](https://betterposters.blogspot.com/2022/07/review-visual-story.html) (the intensity line); study notes for
  the depth-cue list ([Film Book Notes](http://filmbooknotes.blogspot.com/2013/05/the-visual-story-by-bruce-block.html),
  [chapter 3 flashcards](https://quizlet.com/98223755/the-visual-story-chapter-3-space-flash-cards/)); the exact wording
  of the principle as a cinematographer teaches it ([C. J. Rajkumar, 2020](https://thesica.in/contrast-cj-rajkumar/):
  "The greater the contrast in a visual component, the more the visual intensity or dynamic increases").
- **What it means for us.** This is the missing middle level. "A feel of distance" is an intent; *space* is the
  component; the depth cues are the tools; how many cues and how strong is the dial. A guide written this way never
  says where a building goes, and still cannot forget anything: the seven components (plus our words and our people)
  are the complete list of what a frame is made of, so a frame card that asks one question per component covers the
  whole frame. Intensity becomes a dial we set per beat (affinity in the calm beats, contrast at the hook and the turn),
  which our colour-script notes already half-say (`look/composition/cinema.md` §7).

**F2. A studio style guide states its rules as balances and feelings, and lets the style pick the depth tools (Bacher, *Mulan*).**
- **The practice.** Hans Bacher's style guides for Disney's *Mulan* (1995, revised 1997) never place anything. They
  give one master rule ("the main rule of the Chinese Style - BALANCE"), then "the 6 most important rules", each a
  pair of poles to balance: "busy and quiet", "straight and curved", "positive and negative space", "clear silhouette
  readability", "empty stage for the action", "avoid overloading the picture with too much information" ("rhythmic
  composition" added in 1997). The same law runs under all of it: "light in front of dark and opposite, or warm in front
  of cold and opposite, or curved in front of straight and opposite, or simple in front of very detailed and opposite
  and so on - always using the contraries." Distance is stated as a feeling and its tool, in almost the owner's words:
  "Separate the background into several detail-levels, starting with nearly no detail in the mountain-level, like they
  were in the mist, the closer you move to the fore-ground and to were your focus-level is, the more detail you use";
  "Simplification means just enough to feel the scale and the distance"; "create different layers with use of
  out-of-focus levels or more contrast in color in the foreground and more subdued in the background". The style also
  chooses WHICH depth tools it uses: "avoid excessive use of perspective - there is no perspective in Chinese Art.
  Create depth by defining the focus levels." Intents are written as feelings ("it should look light and fresh", "Show
  a wealthy environment, a bit like a boudoir", "Try to create emotion in your compositions. Like fear - someone is
  trapped", "Intimidating guardians - make the look dramatic by using light from below ... clear size-difference"), and
  the choice of tools follows the emotion and the neighbours: "It all depends on what kind of emotion you have to
  create following the story and if your scene works in connection with the preceeding and following shots." The
  pictures in the guide are examples (layouts corrected with the reason, "The following scenes from 'Cinderella' will
  show you how well planned every single composition was"), never templates. And it plans in small, cheap pictures
  first: "You get the best feeling for composition and contrast-values in small thumbnail-sketches. Try to use a
  limited amount of Grey's or Blue's - not more than 4."
- **Source and strength.** A: the guides themselves, scanned on the Internet Archive
  ([1995](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1995), [1997](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1997_202405);
  quotes from their OCR text, spelling as printed), and the 1997 text on the
  [Disney Index Project](https://www.dix-project.net/document/misc-documents_1997-04-05_mulan-style-guide). B/C: the
  history in [Animation Obsessive, "Setting the Stage for 'Mulan'" (2024)](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/setting-the-stage-for-mulan),
  which also reports that Bacher "corrected hundreds of layouts and backgrounds" and that the film used the rules more
  softly than the guide.
- **What it means for us.** This is the format the owner is asking for, already proven on a feature: a few balances
  (each a dial with two poles), the feeling of each place in plain words, the tools a feeling can use, and the style's
  own choice of which tools it allows (Mulan: depth by detail and focus levels and mist, not by perspective). For us
  "a feel of distance" is the same sentence, and the style decides its tools (in our painted look: paler, cooler, less
  detailed and lower-contrast layers, never blur alone). Two working habits come with it: tiny value thumbnails (at
  most 4 values) before any frame is built, and a production designer who corrects frames by naming the broken
  principle, which is a role our process lacks (F11).

**F3. The same elements can say opposite things; the camera and the light carry the message (Mateu-Mestre, *Framed Ink*).**
- **The practice.** Marcos Mateu-Mestre (story and layout artist on *Balto*, *The Prince of Egypt*, *Surf's Up*) teaches
  composition as a way to "make the audience 'feel' the story while they are 'reading' the story", from a single image
  to "continuity between all the shots", worked through three stories (a train accident, a cowboy tale, bikers at a
  mysterious house). The book's own contents name the method: "Using the same elements we can deliver very different
  messages"; "Cowboys Part 1: Keeping the camera on the same spot"; "Cowboys Part 2: Changing the camera position with
  a purpose". Reviewers report the same image redrawn with other light or angles "to show how much of a dramatic
  difference it causes", and the tools it uses as "complexity and simplicity, light and dark, size and distance and
  point of view".
- **Source and strength.** A: the publisher's description ([Design Studio Press](https://designstudiopress.com/products/framed-ink));
  the contents as a library catalogue lists them (through the search index of
  [AUT library](https://librarysearch.aut.ac.nz/vufind/Record/1828269/TOC); the page itself refused a direct read).
  C: [CG Channel review (2010)](https://www.cgchannel.com/2010/11/book-review-framed-ink);
  [C. Parker, Lines and Colors (2010)](https://linesandcolors.com/?p=3457).
- **What it means for us.** The owner's "BILLION ways" is the book's premise: the content (the houses, the street) is
  free; what the frame must get right is the message, carried by a few chosen tools (light and dark, size and distance,
  point of view, simplicity against complexity). So a guide names the message and the tools, and every artist picks
  the elements. A frame is judged by "does it say the beat's feeling?", never by "is the building where the example
  put it?". And "with a purpose" applies to every camera change: a move or a new angle must name the feeling it adds.

**F4. The colour script plans the feeling of every beat in one strip, in colour, value and drama, never in objects (Pixar).**
- **The practice.** At Pixar the colour script came in with Ralph Eggleston (art director on *Toy Story*, production
  designer on *WALL-E* and *Inside Out*): one long strip of small pastel frames for the whole film. In the foreword of
  *The Art of Pixar: The Complete Colorscripts* he calls it "a kind of 'stream of consciousness' map of color, value,
  and visual drama"; the book's reviewer sums it as "Color scripts map out the emotional beats of a story", so that
  everyone can "understand the vision as a whole". Eggleston's own account of the method: "I'm an intuitive artist,
  and can only work from a story point of view"; "doodling with little blobs of color all over the place"; "Caricaturing
  the lighting to support the characters emotions"; and a test of a world's colour plan is "the audience's ability to
  tell where you are on the ship at a moment's notice, because of the color and lighting" (*WALL-E*). He works by
  questions: "Once you figure out what questions to ask you can begin answering them one at a time."
- **Source and strength.** B: R. Barbagallo, ["Design With a Purpose", an interview with Ralph Eggleston (2008-2009)](https://www.animationartconservation.com/design-with-a-purpose,-an-interview-with-ralph-eggleston.html).
  A (quoted in C): Eggleston's foreword, quoted by [A. Solarin, Hyperallergic (2021)](https://hyperallergic.com/the-art-of-pixar-chronicle-books/).
- **What it means for us.** The colour script is a beat sheet of feelings made visible: one cell per beat, each cell
  saying its mood with light, colour and value, never with objects; and the strip shows the whole film's curve at
  once, so a calm beat and the turn can be set against each other (F1's dial, over time). Our colour scripts drifted
  into object plans (video 4's strip showed lone houses and the owner read it as the scene being empty): the strip
  should fix the feeling, the key and the value plan per beat; the content belongs to the key frames. It is also the
  cheapest place to catch a missing component for the film as a whole.

**F5. Line and shape are given a meaning per world and per character, and a "rule book" says WHEN to use a tool, not where (Cartoon Saloon, Pixar).**
- **The practice.** On *Wolfwalkers* (Cartoon Saloon, 2020) the town is drawn in "a very uniform kind of woodblock
  line that showed their mindset", "the forest animals and the Wolfwalkers would be more scratchy", and the heroine's
  "line work would change over the course of the movie" (T. Moore). The character designer: "a very loose style in the
  forest that would evoke freedom"; the town "is very rigid" (F. Pirovano). Colour follows the same idea: "giving the
  lower colors to the townspeople. Then the more vibrant reds and very passionate colors for the wild in the forest"
  (M. Pareja, production designer). The directors wrote a storyboard "manifesto" and a "little rule book about when we
  would use depth, when we would use triptychs and split screen"; a frame's shape answers the character's feeling: "if
  we wanted to say draw a character like Mebh who was feeling enclosed, we might draw a big square box around her"
  (R. Stewart). Clean-up artists were asked to consider "what was going on for the character" in each shot. And the
  team was not asked to copy a frame: "each artist is putting their stamp on it" (Pareja); the background layouts were
  to be "as scribbly and rough as possible" (Stewart). At Pixar, *Inside Out*'s character art director Albert Lozano
  started "with very basic shape language", each shape chosen from a feeling: "Joy felt like a star", "Anger felt like
  a square", "Disgust felt triangular and kind of pointed", "Sadness just felt sort of like a blue teardrop"; and the
  two worlds are two settings of the same dials: "Riley's real world is stylistically vertical with straight lines and
  realistic shapes and shading", while "the world of Riley's mind has organic and simple shapes that glow in
  translucent materials and feature more open spaces".
- **Source and strength.** B: [I. Failes, befores & afters (2020)](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/12/16/wolfwalkers-and-the-art-of-staging-shots/)
  (the directors' own words); [S. Prusakowski, Awards Radar (2021)](https://awardsradar.com/2021/03/04/interview-wolfwalkers-artists-on-creating-magic-between-the-lines/)
  (the artists' own words); [ACM SIGGRAPH blog, "Inside the Minds Behind 'Inside Out'" (2016)](https://blog.siggraph.org/2016/02/inside-the-minds-behind-inside-out.html/)
  (Lozano's own words).
- **What it means for us.** Two lessons. First, a rule is written as WHEN (the feeling) -> WHICH tool: "when the
  viewer should feel enclosed, frame the subject in a box", "when the world should feel wide, open the depth" — never
  "put the box at x". Our composition library's devices are tools; each needs its "use it when the beat feels ..." line
  (and its "never when ..."). Second, the line, the shape and the colour each carry a meaning across the film (the
  world of the system against the world of the person, as a dial); the film declares its few meanings once, and every
  frame obeys them while inventing its own content.
