The story is three days old, so recheck exploitation status and counts on the day of publishing.

**The 12 most important verified facts, with wording we can safely say:**

1. **Countries:** "Governments in at least eight countries, from France and Germany to the UK, Australia and Japan, pulled their own websites offline." The eight with solid sourcing are the Netherlands, Finland, France, Belgium, Germany, the Czech Republic, the UK and Australia. Japan is in the Reuters factbox only. All of this was on Wednesday 21 Oct; most sites were back within hours to a day.
2. **No hack:** "None of them says it was hacked. They pulled the plug as a precaution, many before the fix even existed." The fix came out around 14:00 UTC on Oct 21, and most went dark that morning.
3. **What the bug does:** "Send the right web request and a stranger can make the website's server run their own code. No password, no click from anyone." It can also leak leftover memory holding passwords and log-in tokens. Wiz demonstrated it on WordPress, Drupal and Joomla.
4. **How common PHP is:** "PHP runs about 7 in 10 websites." Put a footnote on screen: W3Techs, 69.3% of sites whose server-side language is known. WordPress, which is built on PHP, runs 39.6% of all websites.
5. **How old it is:** "The flaw had been sitting in PHP since 2012. PHP's own developers even spotted the weak spot in a 2024 audit, but nobody could prove it was dangerous or agree on a fix." The PHP Foundation now calls this a "structural failure".
6. **Who found it:** "In August, two security teams, both using AI models, found it independently." Calif used OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol and reported on Aug 11. Wiz used its own Wiz Atlas tool plus Google's Gemini and reported on Aug 24. "AI helped find" is safer than "AI found".
7. **The leak:** "They told PHP privately so a fix could be ready first. But word leaked a day early and the fix was rushed out." What leaked on Oct 20 was the bug ID numbers (with a wrong severity score) and a video of an exploit working. The fix followed about 18 to 22 hours later.
8. **Attacks before the fix:** "Four days before the flaw was announced, Amazon's decoy servers caught someone already trying it." That was Oct 17, and Amazon says it can't tell how the attacker knew.
9. **Cloudflare's numbers:** "Cloudflare, which sits in front of about 1 in 5 websites, says its network saw tens of thousands of attempts in the first 45 minutes after the announcement, and millions within the day." These come from its CEO's post. They are attempts, mostly blocked, not break-ins. There were also "thousands" in the 10 hours before the announcement.
10. **Official advice:** "The FBI's advice was literally: if you can't update right away, take it offline." The NSA, the UK's National Cyber Security Centre, Germany's federal cyber agency (BSI) and Japan's prime minister said the same thing.
11. **Patching is slow:** "A day after the fix came out, more than 99 in 100 PHP servers you could check still hadn't updated." OX Security found 14,151 of 1,974,890 showing a patched version. This is based on reported version numbers, so it overstates somewhat.
12. **Old versions:** "At least 1 in 3 PHP websites run a version PHP no longer fixes." PHP 7 and older is 34.7% of PHP sites (W3Techs). Adding PHP 8.0 and 8.1, which are also unsupported, gets to roughly half. The 8.0/8.1 share is an estimate from July data. Optional true detail: "Updating WordPress won't fix it; the fix has to come from whoever runs the server."

**What's wrong or misleading in the current script:**

- **"four countries"** is wrong. It's at least eight.
- **"for 14 years, nobody did [find it]"** is misleading. The code is about 14 years old, but the weak spot was flagged in 2020 and spotted again in 2024, then left unfixed because nobody proved it was exploitable.
- **"the details of the bug leaked online hours before the fix"** is misleading. The ID numbers and a video leaked about a day before, and PHP says no technical details leaked. An attempt was seen even earlier, on Oct 17.
- **"22 million copies of PHP"** is misleading. Palo Alto Networks counted "22.6 million exposed instances potentially vulnerable." Those are internet-facing services, not copies or websites, and not confirmed vulnerable. Other scanners count anywhere from 2M to 41M. Say "more than 22 million exposed PHP systems, by one firm's count."
- **"one in three sites on PHP"** is true but understated. Including PHP 8.0 and 8.1 it's about half. Nuance: PHP did publish source-only fixes for 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1, and Linux distributions and hosts ship patched builds.
- **"switch off or hope the hosting company sorts it out"** leaves out the real fix, which is upgrading to PHP 8.2 or later.
- **"break-in attempts… millions by evening"** is fine only as "attempts", never as break-ins. "Within the day" is safer than "by evening".
- **"Nobody hacked them"** is OK for the governments that went offline. But don't widen it to "no government was hacked": Palo Alto's researchers describe one unnamed European government body where attackers got password data within two hours.

**"PHP is the most popular programming language in the world" is false.** Python leads the TIOBE index (PHP is around #12). JavaScript leads the Stack Overflow survey (PHP is about 19% of developers). TypeScript leads GitHub's 2025 report. True alternatives:
- "PHP is the most-used language for running websites, and it's not close: about 7 in 10 sites. The runner-up, JavaScript, is about 1 in 12."
- "It runs more of the web than every other server language combined." Footnote this one, because some sites use more than one language.
- "It's the code behind Wikipedia and WordPress, which alone runs about 4 in 10 websites."

**Visuals:** almost all of the new facts fit the city world as it is: inspectors spotting the crack in the blueprint in 2024, a decoy building for Amazon's trap, a city where only a few windows light up as fixed, half the buildings on an old blueprint. Japan's PM and the FBI warning would need a new billboard or loudspeaker asset, with no real faces, seals or logos. The Cloudflare dome should cover about a fifth to a quarter of the skyline, not the whole city, to match the 1-in-5 fact.

Governments across Europe switched off their own websites on purpose this week. They did it because of a flaw that can let a stranger take over a website without a password. It's in PHP, the language everyone loves, and about seven in ten websites run on it. That flaw had been sitting there for fourteen years, until researchers using AI figured out how bad it was.
