# Research: how art direction writes principles as feelings and tools, never placements (2026-10-30)

**Status:** done (14 findings, the model). Research notes: the lowest rank on DIRECTION's ladder; nothing here is a
rule until the design language, the frame card or the rework integrates it.

**The question (BRIEF.md, the owner):** our guides mix feelings, tools, concrete placements and numbers; agents copy
placements (no imagination) or miss whole areas (incomplete frames). How do professionals state "a feel of distance"
and its tools (layers) so every artist invents the frame, and still nothing is forgotten?

**Strength key:** A = the author's own book or document, or a studio's own published account (its guide, its site);
B = the practitioner's own words in a talk, course, workshop or interview; C = a secondary write-up (a review, a
summary, notes taken by someone else, a quote passed on). "A (quoted in C)" = the author's own words, read through a
secondary page.

## Findings

**F1. A feeling is made by a small set of visual components, each a dial between contrast and affinity (Block).**
- **The practice.** Bruce Block (*The Visual Story*, 3rd ed. 2020) says every picture is built from seven basic visual
  components: space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement and rhythm. The publisher describes the book as using them to
  "convey moods and emotions, create a visual style", and says "the connection between story and visual structures"
  guides the choices of camera, staging, lighting and editing. Each component runs from affinity (alike) to contrast
  (different): "Visual structure is based on an understanding of the Principle of Contrast & Affinity." Contrast in a
  component raises the visual intensity; affinity lowers it ("Contrast increases visual intensity, but affinity
  decreases visual intensity"). Space has four kinds (deep, flat, limited, ambiguous), and deep space is made by depth
  cues: perspective, size difference, relative movement, textural diffusion, aerial diffusion, shape change, tonal
  separation, colour separation, up/down position, overlap and focus. The book's own order: the components, then
  "Story & Visual Structures", then "Practice, Not Theory"; "picture makers must understand story structure or they
  may never be able to correctly use visual structure".
- **Source and strength.** A: the publisher's description and contents
  ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story-Creating-the-Visual-Structure-of-Film-TV-and-Digital-Media/Block/p/book/9781138014152));
  the two quoted lines of the book through the search index of the
  [O'Reilly chapter 2](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9780240807799/009_9780080551692_chapter2.html)
  and [chapter 9](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9781317745693/xhtml/Ch09.xhtml) previews (the
  pages themselves refused a direct read, HTTP 403). C: Z. Faulkes's
  [review](https://betterposters.blogspot.com/2022/07/review-visual-story.html) (the intensity line); study notes for
  the depth-cue list and the camera's part in it ("Dolly in creates more depth"; a pan, a tilt and a zoom keep space
  flat) ([Film Book Notes, 2013](http://filmbooknotes.blogspot.com/2013/05/the-visual-story-by-bruce-block.html)); the exact wording
  of the principle as a cinematographer teaches it ([C. J. Rajkumar, 2020](https://thesica.in/contrast-cj-rajkumar/):
  "The greater the contrast in a visual component, the more the visual intensity or dynamic increases").
- **What it means for us.** This is the missing middle level. "A feel of distance" is an intent; *space* is the
  component; the depth cues are the tools; how many cues and how strong is the dial. A guide written this way never
  says where a building goes, and still cannot forget anything: the seven components (plus our words and our people)
  are the complete list of what a frame is made of, so a frame card that asks one question per component covers the
  whole frame. Intensity becomes a dial we set per beat (affinity in the calm beats, contrast at the hook and the turn),
  which our colour-script notes already half-say (`look/composition/cinema.md` §7).

**F2. A studio style guide states its rules as balances and feelings, and lets the style pick the depth tools (Bacher, *Mulan*).**
- **The practice.** Hans Bacher's style guides for Disney's *Mulan* (1995, revised 1997) never place anything. They
  give one master rule ("the main rule of the Chinese Style - BALANCE"), then "the 6 most important rules", each a
  pair of poles to balance: "busy and quiet", "straight and curved", "positive and negative space", "clear silhouette
  readability", "empty stage for the action", "avoid overloading the picture with too much information" ("rhythmic
  composition" added in 1997). The same law runs under all of it: "light in front of dark and opposite, or warm in front
  of cold and opposite, or curved in front of straight and opposite, or simple in front of very detailed and opposite
  and so on - always using the contraries." Distance is stated as a feeling and its tool, in almost the owner's words:
  "Separate the background into several detail-levels, starting with nearly no detail in the mountain-level, like they
  were in the mist, the closer you move to the fore-ground and to were your focus-level is, the more detail you use";
  "Simplification means just enough to feel the scale and the distance"; "create different layers with use of
  out-of-focus levels or more contrast in color in the foreground and more subdued in the background". The style also
  chooses WHICH depth tools it uses: "avoid excessive use of perspective - there is no perspective in Chinese Art.
  Create depth by defining the focus levels." Intents are written as feelings ("it should look light and fresh", "Show
  a wealthy environment, a bit like a boudoir", "Try to create emotion in your compositions. Like fear- someone is
  trapped", "Intimidating guardians - make the look dramatic by using light from below ... clear size-difference"), and
  the choice of tools follows the emotion and the neighbours: "It all depends on what kind of emotion you have to
  create following the story and if your scene works in connection with the preceeding and following shots." The
  pictures in the guide are examples (layouts corrected with the reason, "The following scenes from 'Cinderella' will
  show you how well planned every single composition was"), never templates. And it plans in small, cheap pictures
  first: "You get the best feeling for composition and contrast-values in small thumbnail-sketches. Try to use a
  limited amount of Grey's or Blue's - not more than 4." His book *Dream Worlds* (2008) teaches the same layers with
  their values chosen by the feeling, not by nature: "We reduce these middle tones to only three ... Define the
  foreground, one or two middle ground areas, and then the background. In case you want to arrange the values
  realistically, your foreground might be the darkest, and your background the lightest tone. But even in nature you
  will find a situation where the sky is the darkest value and a middle-ground the lightest. You should know what you
  want to design without following the rules of nature. To achieve the desired result, a scary moment or a romantic
  scene, you choose the best arrangement of values"; and, for film's few seconds a shot: "Nothing is accidental. We
  lead the eyes of the audience."
- **Source and strength.** A: the guides themselves, scanned on the Internet Archive
  ([1995](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1995), [1997](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1997_202405);
  quotes from their OCR text, spelling as printed), and the 1997 text on the
  [Disney Index Project](https://www.dix-project.net/document/misc-documents_1997-04-05_mulan-style-guide). A (quoted
  in C): *Dream Worlds: Production Design for Animation* (Focal Press, 2008), quoted at length by
  [World of Level Design, "11 Things I Learned from Dream Worlds" (2014)](https://www.worldofleveldesign.com/categories/game_environments_design/11-things-i-learned-from-dream-worlds.php).
  B/C: the history in [Animation Obsessive, "Setting the Stage for 'Mulan'" (2024)](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/setting-the-stage-for-mulan),
  which also reports that Bacher "corrected hundreds of layouts and backgrounds" and that the film used the rules more
  softly than the guide.
- **What it means for us.** This is the format the owner is asking for, already proven on a feature: a few balances
  (each a dial with two poles), the feeling of each place in plain words, the tools a feeling can use, and the style's
  own choice of which tools it allows (Mulan: depth by detail and focus levels and mist, not by perspective). For us
  "a feel of distance" is the same sentence, and the style decides its tools (in our painted look: layers that step
  toward the air's colour, simpler and softer in contrast with distance, never a plane that is only blurred). The
  value arrangement of the layers is itself a choice made for the feeling, never a law of nature. Two working habits
  come with it: tiny value thumbnails (3-4 values) before any frame is built, and a production designer who corrects
  frames by naming the broken principle, which is a role our process lacks (F12).

**F3. The same elements can say opposite things; the camera and the light carry the message (Mateu-Mestre, *Framed Ink*).**
- **The practice.** Marcos Mateu-Mestre (on *Balto*, *The Prince of Egypt* and *Surf's Up*, per the publisher, "frame
  composition, lighting and visual continuity" for key sequences) teaches
  composition as a way to "make the audience 'feel' the story while they are 'reading' the story", from a single image
  to "continuity between all the shots", worked through three stories (a train accident, a cowboy tale, bikers at a
  mysterious house). The book's own contents name the method: "Using the same elements we can deliver very different
  messages"; "Cowboys Part 1: Keeping the camera on the same spot"; "Cowboys Part 2: Changing the camera position with
  a purpose". Reviewers report the same image redrawn with other light or angles "to show how much of a dramatic
  difference it causes", and the tools it uses as "complexity and simplicity, light and dark, size and distance and
  point of view".
- **Source and strength.** A: the publisher's description ([Design Studio Press](https://designstudiopress.com/products/framed-ink));
  the contents as a library catalogue lists them (through the search index of
  [AUT library](https://librarysearch.aut.ac.nz/vufind/Record/1828269/TOC); the page itself refused a direct read).
  C: [CG Channel review (2010)](https://www.cgchannel.com/2010/11/book-review-framed-ink);
  [C. Parker, Lines and Colors (2010)](https://linesandcolors.com/?p=3457).
- **What it means for us.** The owner's "BILLION ways" is the book's premise: the content (the houses, the street) is
  free; what the frame must get right is the message, carried by a few chosen tools (light and dark, size and distance,
  point of view, simplicity against complexity). So a guide names the message and the tools, and every artist picks
  the elements. A frame is judged by "does it say the beat's feeling?", never by "is the building where the example
  put it?". And "with a purpose" applies to every camera change: a move or a new angle must name the feeling it adds.

**F4. The colour script plans the feeling of every beat in one strip, in colour, value and drama, never in objects (Pixar).**
- **The practice.** At Pixar the colour script came in with Ralph Eggleston (art director on *Toy Story*, production
  designer on *WALL-E* and *Inside Out*): one long strip of small pastel frames for the whole film. In the foreword of
  *The Art of Pixar: The Complete Colorscripts* he calls it "a kind of 'stream of consciousness' map of color, value,
  and visual drama"; the book's reviewer sums it as "Color scripts map out the emotional beats of a story", so that
  everyone can "understand the vision as a whole". Eggleston's own account of the method: "I'm an intuitive artist,
  and can only work from a story point of view"; "doodling with little blobs of color all over the place"; "Caricaturing
  the lighting to support the characters emotions"; and a test of a world's colour plan is "the audience's ability to
  tell where you are on the ship at a moment's notice, because of the color and lighting" (*WALL-E*). He works by
  questions: "Once you figure out what questions to ask you can begin answering them one at a time."
- **Source and strength.** B: R. Barbagallo, ["Design With a Purpose", an interview with Ralph Eggleston (2008-2009)](https://www.animationartconservation.com/design-with-a-purpose,-an-interview-with-ralph-eggleston.html).
  A (quoted in C): Eggleston's foreword, quoted by [A. Solarin, Hyperallergic (2021)](https://hyperallergic.com/the-art-of-pixar-chronicle-books/).
- **What it means for us.** The colour script is a beat sheet of feelings made visible: one cell per beat, each cell
  saying its mood with light, colour and value, never with objects; and the strip shows the whole film's curve at
  once, so a calm beat and the turn can be set against each other (F1's dial, over time). Our colour scripts drifted
  into object plans (video 4's strip showed lone houses and the owner read it as the scene being empty): the strip
  should fix the feeling, the key and the value plan per beat; the content belongs to the key frames. It is also the
  cheapest place to catch a missing component for the film as a whole.

**F5. Line and shape are given a meaning per world and per character, and a "rule book" says WHEN to use a tool, not where (Cartoon Saloon, Pixar).**
- **The practice.** On *Wolfwalkers* (Cartoon Saloon, 2020) the town is drawn in "a very uniform kind of woodblock
  line that showed their mindset", "the forest animals and the Wolfwalkers would be more scratchy", and the heroine's
  "line work would change over the course of the movie" (T. Moore). The character designer: "a very loose style in the
  forest that would evoke freedom"; the town "is very rigid" (F. Pirovano). Colour follows the same idea: "giving the
  lower colors to the townspeople. Then the more vibrant reds and very passionate colors for the wild in the forest"
  (M. Pareja, production designer). The directors wrote a storyboard "manifesto" and a "little rule book about when we
  would use depth, when we would use triptychs and split screen"; a frame's shape answers the character's feeling: "if
  we wanted to say draw a character like Mebh who was feeling enclosed, we might draw a big square box around her"
  (R. Stewart). Clean-up artists were asked to consider "what was going on for the character" in each shot. And the
  team was not asked to copy a frame: "each artist is putting their stamp on it" (Pareja); the background layouts were
  to be "as scribbly and rough as possible" (Stewart). At Pixar, *Inside Out*'s character art director Albert Lozano
  started "with very basic shape language", each shape chosen from a feeling: "Joy felt like a star", "Anger felt like
  a square", "Disgust felt triangular and kind of pointed", "Sadness just felt sort of like a blue teardrop"; and the
  two worlds are two settings of the same dials: "Riley's real world is stylistically vertical with straight lines and
  realistic shapes and shading", while "the world of Riley's mind has organic and simple shapes that glow in
  translucent materials and feature more open spaces".
- **Source and strength.** B: [I. Failes, befores & afters (2020)](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/12/16/wolfwalkers-and-the-art-of-staging-shots/)
  (the directors' own words); [S. Prusakowski, Awards Radar (2021)](https://awardsradar.com/2021/03/04/interview-wolfwalkers-artists-on-creating-magic-between-the-lines/)
  (the artists' own words); [ACM SIGGRAPH blog, "Inside the Minds Behind 'Inside Out'" (2016)](https://blog.siggraph.org/2016/02/inside-the-minds-behind-inside-out.html/)
  (Lozano's own words).
- **What it means for us.** Two lessons. First, a rule is written as WHEN (the feeling) -> WHICH tool: "when the
  viewer should feel enclosed, frame the subject in a box", "when the world should feel wide, open the depth" — never
  "put the box at x". Our composition library's devices are tools; each needs its "use it when the beat feels ..." line
  (and its "never when ..."). Second, the line, the shape and the colour each carry a meaning across the film (the
  world of the system against the world of the person, as a dial); the film declares its few meanings once, and every
  frame obeys them while inventing its own content.

**F6. Depth is a dial the story sets, not a quality every frame maximises; two worlds are two settings of the same dials (Laika, Fortiche).**
- **The practice.** On *Coraline* (Laika, 2009) Henry Selick held depth back on purpose: "The sets are built with less
  depth because the story is about her dissatisfaction and loneliness at home"; "Coraline's life is less dimensional,
  so there is a muted color palette"; he wanted to "save 3D for the other world, to draw people into the 3D space". The
  same room, two settings: "In the real world, the living room has a crushed perspective, very little actual depth to
  it"; in the Other World "what looks to be the same living room, but it's built very, very deep", so that "you can
  really breathe in that other place". On *Arcane* the production designer describes the same move with shape:
  Piltover got "a completely new shape language based on geometry, symmetry, and mechanical beauty", then "Zaun needed
  to be its dark mirror", with upcycled ornaments to "bring some organic beauty to the poor and toxic undercity".
  (*Inside Out*'s two worlds, F5, are the same idea.)
- **Source and strength.** B: S. Macaulay, ["Henry Selick In Conversation", Focus Features (2009)](https://www.focusfeatures.com/article/henry_selick_in_conversation);
  [Groucho Reviews interview (2009)](https://www.grouchoreviews.com/interviews/6334f8475fe75d1811c62762);
  A.-L. Baudry interviewed by A. Sergeev, [80.lv (2023)](https://80.lv/articles/riot-games-on-designing-arcane-s-piltover-zaun).
- **What it means for us.** "A feel of distance" is right for some beats and wrong for others: wide, deep space for
  wonder, scale and breathing room; limited space (a near wall, a shallow room, one plane) for closeness, confinement or
  focus. Our guides treat depth as a must for every shot (`look/CRAFT.md` §f: "every shot reads in distance"); the
  research keeps separation in every frame (the subject never melts into what is behind it), but lets the beat set the
  AMOUNT of depth, and a film can climb from limited to deep space as its story opens (Block's progression, F1).
  Limited space is a choice a beat makes on purpose and names on its card, never a frame that reads flat by accident
  (the owner's v2 note behind CRAFT §f stands). Where a film has two sides (the system and the person, the problem and
  the home), it declares them once as two settings of the same dials (shape, line, colour, space, light), and every
  frame takes its side's setting.

**F7. Light has a short, complete list of jobs, and it makes depth by planes; the principles are a vocabulary, "not rules to be followed" (Calahan, Pixar).**
- **The practice.** Sharon Calahan (Pixar's lighting supervisor on *Toy Story*, later its director of photography for
  lighting) wrote the lighting
  course notes for SIGGRAPH 1996: "The primary purpose of cinematic lighting is storytelling ... it is important to
  understand the story-point behind each shot, and how it relates to the story as a whole. It is not enough that the
  lighting designer simply illuminate the scene so the viewer can see what is happening, or to make it look pretty."
  Light has six jobs (adapted from Ross Lowell's *Matters of Light and Depth*): "Directing the viewer's eye; Enhancing
  mood, atmosphere and drama; Creating depth; Conveying time of day and season; Revealing character personality and
  situation; Complementing composition." On the status of such principles: "They are not rules to be followed, but
  define a structure by which to explore creative possibilities. They describe a visual vocabulary, and provide
  methods for breaking down a complex image into manageable characteristics for subjective analysis." On depth: "This
  implied depth must be created; it does not happen automatically simply because the subject matter is
  three-dimensional." Its tool is planes: "a collection of objects or subjects which are parallel with the camera plane
  and are lit as a unit to contrast with other planes ... for the purposes of creating the illusion of depth through
  layers". Her example: four planes (the view out of a window, the wall, the subjects, a vase of flowers near the
  camera), each "clearly defined by its brightness", the separation "further accentuated by color variations between
  planes" (warm light on the people, cool on the wall), a little backlight, the nearest and farthest out of focus; "By
  finding ways to create planes of light, a scene becomes easier to visually organize. It also gains much added
  richness and beauty."
- **Source and strength.** A: S. Calahan, "Storytelling through Lighting: A Computer Graphics Perspective", in
  *Pixel Cinematography*, ACM SIGGRAPH 1996 Course Notes #30 (copy hosted by a lecturer:
  [terryflaxton.com PDF](https://www.terryflaxton.com/uploads/2/8/9/0/28906783/storytelling_through_lighting_2.pdf)).
- **What it means for us.** Three things. The six jobs are a ready-made light card: every key frame names how its light
  directs the eye, sets the mood, makes depth, tells the hour, reveals the person, and serves the composition. Her
  sentence on principles is the exact status our toolbox needs (a vocabulary for exploring and judging, never a recipe
  for placing). And her depth tool is the owner's "layers": a few planes, each lit and coloured as a unit against its
  neighbours. "It does not happen automatically" names our old failure (v2's "no distance effect", CRAFT §f): we drew
  things at different distances but never separated the planes.

**F8. Value comes first: the frame's "simple statement" in a few big masses, then the detail inside them (Fowkes, Bacher).**
- **The practice.** Nathan Fowkes (DreamWorks colour keys and visual development, e.g. *Shrek the Halls*), in a
  workshop: "Work out the masses of color and value; what is the simple statement?"; "Mass in the values... then the
  architecture with the values"; "If you get the value right... what you do will look solid"; and the colour script
  should be "appropriate for each beat and mood in the script". His own blog says his colour keys exist "to establish
  the mood and lighting of each location". He teaches that the visual storytelling process "can be designed to
  powerfully affect viewers' emotions through the subtle use of color, light, shape and space". Bacher sets the same
  order (F2): small value thumbnails with at most 4 values, the character read as light against dark or dark against
  light, before any layout is drawn.
- **Source and strength.** C (notes of a B event): S. J. Stine, ["Nathan Fowkes Workshop Notes" (Concept Design Academy, 2012)](http://storyduke.blogspot.com/2012/04/nathan-fowkes-workshop-notes.html);
  C: the line quoted by [J. Dougherty, Towncrafting (2014)](https://towncrafting.blogspot.com/2014/01/designing-with-color-and-light-for.html);
  the colour-keys line from [Fowkes's own blog (2007)](http://www.nathanfowkesart.com/2007/11/blog-post_30.html), read
  through the search index (the page returned HTTP 503 here).
- **What it means for us.** A frame is planned whole when its value plan comes first: 3-4 big masses that already say
  the beat's feeling at thumbnail size, with the many small things living inside those masses. This is the answer to
  "never fill for density later": the density is designed as texture inside a planned mass, not added on top of an
  empty one. The check is cheap (the frame reduced to 4 values, or squinted at, still says its statement).

**F9. A style keeps a few constants, translates each tool into its own terms, and frees the artists inside them (Sony Imageworks, *Spider-Verse*).**
- **The practice.** On *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (2018) the studio set a handful of constants and let
  everything else be invented. The constants: "soft gradations are avoided in favor of halftoning and line hatching";
  "One of the rules adopted was avoiding smooth gradations ... if an area transitions, we'd use a dot pattern effect";
  "most of our shadows have some sort of line hatching in them"; animation "on twos" so "each frame appeared as its own
  distinct image, like a panel in a comic book". Depth, the feeling, kept its job but changed its tool to fit the style:
  "Avoiding softness in the film meant avoiding using a traditional lens blur for the camera focus and depth of field",
  replaced by one "inspired by the comic book offset printing process where the (often accidental) mis-registering of
  the color passes can make an image look out of focus". Inside the constants: "Artists were encouraged to experiment
  and try new ideas without concern for how it might break the pipeline"; "the design and style was given more
  importance than accuracy or realism"; "The goal was to encourage people to experiment and to play in hopes of finding
  new ideas" (D. Dimian, VFX supervisor).
- **Source and strength.** A: [Sony Pictures Imageworks, the film's page](https://www.imageworks.com/our-craft/feature-animation/movies/spider-man-spider-verse).
  B: I. Failes, ["'If It's Not Broke, Break It'", Cartoon Brew (2018)](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/feature-film/if-its-not-broke-break-it-sony-imageworks-renegade-approach-to-spider-man-into-the-spider-verse-167321.html).
- **What it means for us.** Our constants should be few and visual (the clean line and how it may roughen, the
  painted fills, the palette family, the light rig family, the people's design, the captions), and stated once. Each
  feeling's tool gets our style's translation, as *Mulan* did with depth by focus levels and *Spider-Verse* with depth
  by colour offset: in our painted look, distance is an air step per plane (lighter, softer, simpler, cooler under a
  blue sky), coarser marks and fewer values far away, never a blur or fog laid over the world (DIRECTION rule 4,
  CRAFT §f). Everything that is not a constant or a feeling's translation is the artist's to invent.

**F10. A world is given as a library of parts and a shape language, so every street is new and still belongs (Fortiche, Disney).**
- **The practice.** For *Arcane*, "We based all the creative work of Arcane on guides to make sure our story is
  consistent with the rest of the IP", and "A team of Concept Artists built a visual library of set design elements,
  props, buildings, and stores so every street could feel unique but unified with the art direction and the shape
  language of the city" (Baudry). Bacher's 1997 guide shows why
  the treatment, not the inventory, makes a place: "Compare the following location designs. They use the same
  elements, a house, trees and mountains - but very little differences indicate very different locations around the
  world".
- **Source and strength.** B: A.-L. Baudry, [80.lv (2023)](https://80.lv/articles/riot-games-on-designing-arcane-s-piltover-zaun).
  A: Bacher's [1997 guide](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1997_202405).
- **What it means for us.** A world bible should be a kit, never a layout (the playbook's "world kit", A RICH,
  BEAUTIFUL WORLD): the place's parts (its homes, its street things, its people, its signs of life) drawn as a library,
  plus its shape language and its dial settings. Each key
  frame then composes its own street from the kit. That is how "each house is so alone" is prevented by design: the kit
  makes a crowded, lived place cheap to compose, and the shape language keeps it one world.

**F11. The art director's order: the story point, then the feeling, then the tools, then many small thumbnails in values, then the frame (Bacher, Calahan, Eggleston).**
- **The practice.** Every source above starts from what the shot must say and feel, never from what is in it.
  Calahan: understand "the story-point behind each shot, and how it relates to the story as a whole". Eggleston:
  "Start from the character and then everything else will follow". Bacher lists the tool choices and what decides
  them: "Usually you have to decide in your composition how you fill your picture depending on the story and the action
  you have to stage, to design it symmetric or not, keep the camera low or high, use shadows in a certain direction and
  choose the right camera lens for a wide or closer shot. It all depends on what kind of emotion you have to create
  following the story and if your scene works in connection with the preceeding and following shots." Then cheap
  pictures, several per shot: "In your first very rough Layout sketch and in thumbnails you have to plan the basic
  elements, the perspective, camera-angles and the contrast"; "Test the best composition in small thumbnails and don't
  forget the value-sketches. Try different variations"; "All these different ideas for the same scene should be used
  for discussion and correction". The action first, the world around it: "plan the key-poses first with a very rough
  Layout, then build the background around them". Only then the finished frame (a background painting, a colour key).
- **Source and strength.** A: Bacher's [1995](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1995) and
  [1997](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1997_202405) guides; Calahan's notes (F7). B: the Eggleston
  interview (F4).
- **What it means for us.** Our loop (beats -> ideas -> key frames -> shot plan -> animatic) has the right order but
  skips the middle of each step. Per beat, the ideas step should write two plain lines before any drawing: the story
  point (what the viewer must understand) and the feeling (what they must feel, in everyday words). Each thumbnail then
  names its chosen tools (a few, from the toolbox, with their dial settings: "deep space by 4 layers, affinity in
  line, warm near and cool far") and is drawn in 3-4 values, in variations. The key frame is built from the picked
  thumbnail, and keeps its two lines, so the builder, the reviewer and the owner all know what the frame is FOR.

**F12. A review names what is wrong, missing or unclear, as a principle; it never dictates the fix or a coordinate (Pixar, Disney).**
- **The practice.** Ed Catmull on Pixar's notes: "A good note says what is wrong, what is missing, what isn't clear,
  what makes no sense"; "A good note doesn't make demands; it doesn't even have to include a proposed fix"; the
  Braintrust "should have no authority. Our group could not tell the director what to do". Bacher's corrections in the
  *Mulan* guide do exactly that, naming the broken principle on a real layout: "This Layout would work for a
  'Bierstadt'-Style Picture. It doesn't look Chinese at all. The foreground has as much detail as the background, there
  is no focus-level, the character - Mulan on her horse - gets lost"; "Composition is not clear enough. Don't copy
  reality - go for the interpretation and don't forget our Style rules"; "Don't make that tree too detailed, the roots
  were creating the wrong interest". One person held the look for the whole team: Bacher "corrected hundreds of
  layouts and backgrounds" (F2). On *Wolfwalkers* even the clean-up artists were asked what "was going on for the
  character" in each shot (F5).
- **Source and strength.** A (quoted in C): E. Catmull, *Creativity, Inc.* (2014), as quoted in
  [S. I. Rascol, "Good Notes" (2015)](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/8136183-good-notes); the Braintrust
  line from Catmull's Stanford talk as reported by [First Round Review (2015)](https://review.firstround.com/spark-creativity-with-these-tips-from-pixars-president/).
  A: Bacher's guides.
- **What it means for us.** Our reviews and our owner notes should be stored as the broken principle plus the example
  that showed it, so the next frame learns the principle and not a patch. "Each house is so alone" is the principle
  "a place is a crowd of lived homes, never objects standing apart" (tools: overlap, shared walls, rows, the street's
  life), never "add 12 houses". A reviewer asks the card's questions (F13) and the artist chooses the fix. One reviewer
  role holds the look across all frames of a film, as a production designer does; checks and lints catch mistakes
  under it, they never stand in for it.

**F13. "Nothing forgotten" comes from short, complete lists asked of every frame, plus one strip for the whole film (Block, Calahan, Bacher, Pixar).**
- **The practice.** The professionals' lists are short and closed, so a frame can be walked through all of it: Block's
  seven components (F1: space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm: everything a picture is made of);
  Calahan's six jobs of light (F7); Bacher's balances (F2: busy and quiet, straight and curved, positive and negative,
  silhouette, empty stage, information load, rhythm) and the thumbnail's must-decide list (the basic elements, the
  perspective, the camera angle, the contrast, the key poses: F11). Across a film, the colour script (F4) puts every
  beat's feeling in one strip, and Block's chapter 9 ties each component's contrast or affinity to the story's build
  (exposition, climax, resolution), so a plan says, per beat, where each dial sits (film courses teach it as "graphing
  visual structure"; the graph's exact form in the book was not readable here).
- **Source and strength.** A: the books and guides above. C: the course page title "Graphing Visual Structure"
  ([University of Texas RTF 318 wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80021989/Graphing+Visual+Structure);
  its body did not load) and G. Reynolds's endorsement on the publisher's page: the book "uses these three basics of
  story - Exposition, Climax, Resolution - to show the link between visual structure and story structure"
  ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story-Creating-the-Visual-Structure-of-Film-TV-and-Digital-Media/Block/p/book/9781138014152)).
- **What it means for us.** This is our missing safety net, and it costs no placements. A FRAME CARD = these lists
  merged into one set of questions (one per component, one per job of light, one per balance, plus what only we have:
  the words on screen, the people, the voice's moment, the motion); every key frame answers every question in a few
  words, so a whole area (the people's hair, a caption's tone, the light's hour) cannot be skipped. A FILM STRIP = one
  row per beat, one column per dial (feeling, intensity, space, the key, the lead device), so the curve across the film
  is designed at once. The owner's one-by-one catches (empty scenes, missing recipes, bed-head hair, a menacing caption,
  a computer neck) each map to a question the card would have asked.

**F14. Pictures in a guide are labelled examples with their reason, several per principle, and the guide is applied with judgement (Disney, Cartoon Saloon).**
- **The practice.** The *Mulan* guides teach with captioned pictures, never templates: frames of another film chosen
  for one principle each ("Interesting use of perspective to show that Cinderella is lost in that huge palace, - but all
  the composition lines lead you to her"; "Use of big foreground element for framing and placement of character in the
  middle of pool of light"), and wrong/right pairs with the reason ("here you can compare how boring the > Right < drawn
  version looks"; a "Detail Arrangement" sequence from "monotone, no variety, too flat" to "that's it - it creates a
  center of interest"). The film then applied its own guide more softly than written (F2). On *Wolfwalkers* "each
  artist is putting their stamp on it" (Pareja), and the guide kept them coherent with "something similar but also
  little different" (S. Ume, compositing).
- **Source and strength.** A: Bacher's [1997 guide](https://archive.org/details/mulan-style-guide-1997_202405). B: the
  [Awards Radar interview](https://awardsradar.com/2021/03/04/interview-wolfwalkers-artists-on-creating-magic-between-the-lines/);
  [Animation Obsessive](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/setting-the-stage-for-mulan) on the softer film.
- **What it means for us.** Every picture, recipe, pixel position or number in our toolbox should carry its label:
  EXAMPLE of a named principle (one of many), with the reason it works, and at least two unlike examples per tool, so
  nobody mistakes one layout for the rule. Numbers stay, but in the CHECKS level (to catch a mistake) or labelled as one
  measured example; never as the instruction.

## The model we should use

Read together, the sources write their guidance on four levels and keep placements and numbers out of the top two. A
rule of ours belongs to exactly one level; a guide paragraph that mixes them (CRAFT §f puts the feeling "every shot
reads in distance", its tools and dozens of plane numbers in one block; the cinema research turned each device into
"placement rules in pixels") is what agents copy or skip.

### The four levels

| level | what it says | written as | never contains | who owns it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. INTENT** | what the viewer must understand and FEEL at this beat; per film, the few meanings (the two sides as two dial settings, F5-F6) | everyday words: "the place goes on and on", "warm and safe inside", "small against something huge", "trapped", "relief" | objects, positions, numbers | the story beats (one story point + one feeling per beat) |
| **2. TOOLS** | the visual components that make a feeling: Block's seven (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm), light's six jobs, plus ours (the words on screen, the people, the sync to the voice) | each tool as a dial between two poles (affinity <-> contrast; flat <-> deep; straight <-> curved; quiet <-> busy; warm <-> cool), with "use it when the beat feels ...", "never when ...", and our style's translation of it | where anything goes | the design language (one entry per tool) |
| **3. EXAMPLES** | pictures, recipes, measured frames, film references | each labelled "EXAMPLE of <tool> for <feeling>, one of many", with the reason it works; at least two unlike examples per tool | the status of a rule | the library (COMPOSITION_SHOTS, recipes, references), relabelled |
| **4. CHECKS** | did the intent land, and is anything missing; then the mistakes | questions first (the frame card); then the measurements (captions, safe zones, contrast, CRAFT's numbers, the lints) | a design decision | the reviewer, then the QA scripts |

Beside the levels sit **the constants**: the few visual facts every frame of a film shares, stated once (F9): today
the painted fills with no outlines (an ink line is tried only on the finished film: playbook INK LINES), the palette
family, the light rig family, the people's design, the type and the caption band, the 9:16 frame at 60 fps, and the
film's one world (playbook ONE FILM). Everything that is not a constant or a feeling's translation is the artist's to
invent (F9, F14). Film references (all of the above) teach a principle and stay in research; the examples in our
toolbox are drawn for the tool, never taken from a film, and no film of ours is ever the bar or the example (playbook,
"No past film as the bar or the example").

### How nothing is forgotten (without placing anything)

1. **The FRAME CARD** (one per key frame, F13): the beat's story point and feeling, then one short answer per question,
   each a chosen tool and its dial in words, never a coordinate. SPACE (deep, flat or limited; which depth tools) -
   LINE (the dominant direction; straight or curved; calm or active) - SHAPE (the shape language; whose side of the
   film) - TONE (the 3-4 value masses; where the lightest light meets the darkest dark) - COLOUR (the key; warm and
   cool; a few colours in harmony around the one accent; the side's palette) - MOVEMENT (the one mover; the camera's
   move and what it does to space) - RHYTHM (busy against quiet; big, medium and small shapes; repetition and its
   break) - EDGES (where they are crisp, where they soften or are lost) - LIGHT (the six jobs: where the eye goes, the
   mood, the depth, the hour, what it reveals about the person, how it serves the composition) - THE WORLD (which parts
   of the place are in view; is it lived; the three scales of detail and the world going on past the edges, playbook
   A RICH, BEAUTIFUL WORLD) - THE PEOPLE (who, doing what, feeling what; their design checks) - THE WORDS (the
   caption's tone and size; the voice's word at this moment) - THE JOINS (what carries in, what carries out). A
   question with no answer is an area not yet designed; the card is filled while the thumbnail is chosen, not after
   the build.
2. **The FILM STRIP** (one per film, F4, F13): one row per beat, columns for the feeling, the intensity (affinity to
   contrast), the space dial, the key (value, then colour), the lead device and the side; drawn as the value script,
   then the colour script, under the film's one concept. It designs the curve across the film before any frame is
   built, and every frame is judged in it (playbook ONE FILM: the board read in order).
3. **The art director** (F2, F12; playbook ONE FILM): one role per film owns the look and approves every key frame
   against the whole, by the card's questions, naming the broken principle or the missing area ("no focus level: the
   subject gets lost"), never a pixel; an ALMOST comes back as a paint-over on the frame itself with the principle
   written beside it (as Bacher's "Layout-Correction" pages did), and the artist still chooses the fix. Owner notes
   are stored the same way: the principle, plus the frame that showed it.
4. **Briefs carry the whole toolbox by reference** (the design language, the film's constants, the frame card), never a
   hand-picked subset; a brief adds only the beat's story point and feeling. The artist chooses the content and the
   tools (F3, F11).

### The order of work for one beat (F11)

The story point and the feeling (two lines, from the beats) -> the tools chosen from the design language, with their
dials, named on the card -> several thumbnails in 3-4 values, variations of the same intent (F2, F8) -> the pick, by
the card's questions, as part of a whole sequence -> the key frame built whole in the film's look from the world kit,
keeping its two lines and its card (the film's first one is the hero frame, the owner's bar for finish: playbook A
RICH, BEAUTIFUL WORLD) -> the frame loop (playbook): a fresh evaluator judges the feeling first, then the card's
questions; THERE, ALMOST (a paint-over) or NOT THERE (back to the idea) -> then, and only then, the measurements.

### Our example, at all four levels: "a feel of distance"

**1. INTENT.** "The place goes on far beyond the subject: the viewer feels how big and lived-in the world is, and their
own small, calm part in it." *Use it when* a beat sets up the home place or its scale ("everywhere", "across the
city"), reveals something, or gives a breath after a tense beat. *Never when* the beat is about one thing up close,
closeness or confinement: then limited space says it better (F6). *The dial:* from "a little air behind the subject" to
"a whole vista".

**2. TOOLS** (the component is space; tone, colour, light and movement serve it). Pick the three to five that fit the
frame; the dial is how many and how strong. Our style's translation is in brackets.
- **Layers that overlap:** three or more planes, each partly hiding the one behind (the owner's "visual tool of
  layers"; F2, F7).
- **Planes of light:** each plane lit as a unit against its neighbours, light in front of dark and dark in front of
  light (F2, F7).
- **The air:** each farther plane closer to the sky's colour: lighter, softer in contrast, simpler, cooler under a blue
  sky, warmer under a sunset [one flat step per plane in the paint; never a blur or fog laid over the world: DIRECTION
  rule 4, CRAFT §f].
- **Detail by distance:** the subject's plane carries the detail and the contrast; far planes keep few values and few
  marks [the far marks the coarsest, broad and visible] (F2: "nearly no detail in the mountain-level").
- **Size change of repeated things:** rows of homes, windows, lamps, people, smaller with distance (F1).
- **A receding ground and its vanishing point:** converging edges, foreshortening, joints finer with distance; optional
  (a style may refuse perspective, as *Mulan* did, F2).
- **Up and down:** the farther, the nearer the horizon (F1).
- **Warm near, cool far** (or, under a sunset air, far read by value and simplicity) (F1, F7).
- **A framing near mass** at one edge, in shadow [the one optical blur DIRECTION allows] (F14's *Cinderella* example).
- **In motion:** parallax, the near planes moving most [DIRECTION's float]; a push into the space deepens it, while a
  pan, a tilt or a zoom keeps space flat (F1).

**3. EXAMPLES** (each one of many, never the layout; nothing here says where a building goes).
- *EXAMPLE A, a vista:* a hill town at dusk seen from a rise: a dark fence and a lamp cut by one edge; the subject's
  lit street with its people and shop fronts; three rows of roofs behind, each paler and bluer, their windows smaller
  and fewer, the last row one flat shape against the sky. Why it works: overlap, planes of light, the air and size
  change all say "far" at once, and every plane is still part of a lived town.
- *EXAMPLE B, an interior:* a person at a desk at night; a mug in silhouette near the camera, the person in warm lamp
  light, the wall in cool half-shadow, the city bright and small through the window. Why it works: Calahan's four
  planes in our world; distance without leaving the room.
- *EXAMPLE C, from above:* a high view of the neighbourhood: the near blocks rich with street life, farther blocks
  merging into simpler shapes and the air's colour toward the horizon. Why it works: detail by distance and the air
  make a map feel like a place.

**4. CHECKS.** Questions first: at a squint (or in 4 values), how many planes can you count, and does each read as its
own band? Does the farthest plane read as far on its own (paler, softer, simpler, smaller)? Is nothing behind the
subject busier than the subject's plane, and is there one focus level where the subject is? Is every plane a lived
part of the place, never a backdrop or an empty field? In a move, do the planes keep their parallax order and land at
rest? And does this beat need distance at all? Then the measurements, which catch mistakes and never design: CRAFT §f's
plane steps and `scripts/qa/check_depth.py`.
